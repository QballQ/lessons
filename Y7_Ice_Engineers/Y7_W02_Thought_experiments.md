## 1. Lesson plan (one page, for the teacher)

- **Big idea:** Scientists can test an idea in their heads first (a thought experiment), then turn their curiosity into questions that an experiment can answer.
- **Learning goals:**
  - I can tell the difference between a testable and a not testable question.
  - I can write a testable question and a prediction with a reason.
- **Key words:**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| thought experiment | 思想实验 | testing an idea in your head by imagining what would happen |
| prediction | 预测 | what you think will happen before you test it |
| reason | 理由 | why you think something; it starts with "because" |
| testable | 可检验的 | you can answer it by doing an experiment and measuring |
| evidence | 证据 | the facts that help us decide what is true |
| fall | 下落 | to drop down towards the ground |
| gravity | 重力 | the pull that makes things fall towards the Earth |
| air resistance | 空气阻力 | the push of air against something moving through it |

- **Timeline:**

| Phase | Min | Teacher does | Students do | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do Now | 4 | Mystery cups (moved from Week 1) on each table; reminds: shake, listen, feel the weight, do not open | Shake, listen and weigh two sealed cups in the hand; write "I observe ___. I infer ___ because ___." for each cup | Slide 1; mystery cups; Do Now box on worksheet |
| Hook | 3 | Asks: "If I drop a heavy ball and a light ball together, which lands first?" Class vote. Tells the Galileo story opening | Vote (hands up), say a prediction with "because" | Slides 2 to 3 |
| Learn | 7 | Walks through Galileo's storyboard (6 panels); then drops a book and a flat sheet of paper, then a crumpled sheet and the book, then the paper resting on top of the book. Introduces "testable question" | Predict before each drop; explain with air resistance; note what makes a question testable | Slides 4 to 7; Galileo storyboard poster |
| Do | 18 | Hands out question sort cards (8 min), checks sorts; then sets the winter question task (10 min); circulates | Sort 12 cards into testable / not testable; then write three testable winter questions, each with a prediction and a reason | Slides 8 to 9; question sort cards; worksheet Core, Stretch, Challenge |
| Think and Reflect | 8 | Shows "Heavier things fall faster: always, sometimes or never?"; takes answers; collects exit tickets | Pair talk (any language), share in English with a frame; exit ticket; logbook line | Slides 10 to 11; exit ticket; logbook |

- **Tiers:**
  - Core: number the Galileo storyboard captions in order; gap-fill on testable questions with a word bank; tick testable or not testable.
  - Stretch: rewrite a not testable question as a testable one; write three testable winter questions with "Does ___ change ___?" and a prediction "I predict ___ because ___."
  - Challenge: predict and justify what happens when a heavy steel ball and a light plastic ball of the same size are dropped together, and what happens to a hammer and a feather on the Moon.
- **Language support:**
  - Frames: "I observe ___. I infer ___ because ___." "I predict ___ because ___." "This question is testable because we can change ___ and measure ___." "Does ___ change ___?"
  - Chinese talk welcome during the card sort and the always / sometimes / never discussion.
  - Vocabulary traps: "light" means not heavy here (轻), not light from a lamp (光). "Fall" is a verb here (下落), not autumn (American English). "Testable" (可检验的) is not "test" as in an exam (考试).
- **Misconceptions to watch for:**
  - "Heavier things always fall faster." Without air resistance, all objects fall at the same rate. In air, shape and air resistance matter more than weight: the crumpled paper shows this.
  - "Paper falls slowly because it is light." The flat and crumpled sheets have the same mass but fall very differently, so it is the shape (air resistance), not the mass.
  - "A question with 'why' is always testable." "Why" questions are often hard to test directly; "Does ___ change ___?" questions are easier.
- **Safety:**
  - Mystery cups stay sealed; small objects must not go in mouths.
  - The teacher does the book drop onto a table or the floor from about 1 m, standing clear of feet.
- **If time is short:** skip the "paper on the book" drop (keep book vs flat paper and book vs crumpled paper); students write two winter questions instead of three.
- **Assessment and handover links:**
  - Question sort (checked in class; note groups with three or more errors).
  - Three testable questions on the worksheet (feeds Rubric strand 1, Questioning and predicting).
  - Exit ticket: identify the testable question and write a prediction with a reason.

## 4. Answer key (for the teacher)

**Do Now (mystery cups):** accept any observation of sound, weight or movement and a linked inference. Secure example: "I observe it rolls and makes a clicking sound. I infer it is a marble because it is heavy and it rolls." Teacher key for cup contents is on the kit card. Do not reveal the contents to students: the point is that scientists often cannot open the box.

**Core A (order):** 4, 1, 5, 2, 6, 3
(reading down the table: "But the pair is heavier" = 4; "An old idea" = 1; "Slower and faster" = 5; "Galileo imagines" = 2; "Without air" = 6; "He ties them together" = 3)

**Core B:** experiment, evidence, change, measure, opinion. ("change" and "measure" may be accepted in either order only if the sentence still makes sense; the intended order is change then measure.)

**Core C:** 1 testable; 2 not testable; 3 testable; 4 not testable.

**Question sort cards (Section 6a):**
- Testable: T1 salt, T2 sunny window, T3 bigger snowballs, T4 wool scarf, T5 ice in water or air, T6 sawdust in ice.
- Not testable: N1 snow more beautiful, N2 winter best season, N3 snowmen lonely, N4 polar bears happier, N5 most beautiful ice sculpture, N6 longer winter holidays.
- Discussion point: "Do snowmen feel lonely?" is not testable because snowmen cannot feel, and "lonely" cannot be measured. "Should children have longer winter holidays?" is about values (what people think is right), so an experiment cannot answer it.

**Stretch A (examples):** "Does the temperature outside change how fast snow melts?" "Does wearing a hat change how warm my head stays?" Any question with something to change and something to measure.

**Stretch B (Secure examples):**
- "Does salt change how fast ice melts?" I predict salt will make ice melt faster because people put salt on roads in winter.
- "Does the size of a snowball change how long it takes to melt?" I predict a bigger snowball will take longer to melt because it has more snow inside.
- "Does wrapping a cup in wool change how fast warm water cools?" I predict the wool will keep it warm for longer because wool keeps us warm in winter.
- Accept any testable question; the reason must be linked to the prediction (it can come from everyday experience at this stage).

**Challenge A (Secure example):** "I predict they will land at almost the same time. Gravity pulls both balls down. They are the same size and shape, so the air resistance on each is about the same and is very small compared with their weight over 2 m. So the weight difference makes almost no difference." Excellent level adds: "if dropped from a very tall building, the steel ball might land slightly first, because air resistance slows the light ball a little more."

**Challenge B (Secure example):** "On the Moon they land at the same time because there is no air, so there is no air resistance, and gravity makes everything fall at the same rate. In the classroom on Earth, the hammer lands first. The feather falls more slowly because air resistance pushes up on it a lot, while the hammer is hardly slowed. The difference is the air, not the weight."

**Always, sometimes, never (slide 10):** Sometimes. In air, a heavy object can fall faster than a light object with more air resistance (a hammer and a feather on Earth). Two objects of the same shape and size land at almost the same time over a short drop even if one is heavier. With no air (the Moon, a vacuum), heavy and light objects always fall together. So "heavier things fall faster" is not a rule: shape and air resistance are what matter.

**My thinking (guidance):** good answers mention that on Earth we usually see heavy things (stones) falling faster than light things (leaves, feathers) because of air resistance, and that people did not test the idea carefully. Accept Chinese.

**Exit ticket:** 1: "Does salt make ice melt faster?" 2: Secure example: "I predict salt will make ice melt faster because salt is put on icy roads." Any prediction with a linked reason.

## 7. Notes for the incoming teacher

- Insist on "sometimes" for the always / sometimes / never task: the key idea is that air resistance, not weight, makes the difference.
- What students found hard in trial: (leave blank for now)
- Links to next week: the winter questions and the idea of air resistance lead straight into Week 3 paper spinners, where students change one thing (wing length) and measure one thing (fall time).
