## Lesson 1: Threat from disease | 疾病的威胁

Student Book pp. 37 to 38 (9Ca Threat from disease; 9Ca Diseases (types of disease)) | Workbook pp. 27 to 28 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** People live much longer than they did 180 years ago because we understand what causes disease: pathogens, missing nutrients, faulty genes, lifestyle and the body's own immune system.

**Learning goals**
- I can explain how hygiene, diet, immunisation and medicines have helped people live longer.
- I can give examples of the five types of disease and say which ones can be passed from person to person.

**Key words**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| disease | 疾病 | something that makes you ill |
| pathogen | 病原体 | a microorganism that causes disease |
| infectious disease | 传染病 | a disease caused by a pathogen that can pass from person to person (communicable) |
| non-communicable | 非传染性的 | cannot be passed from person to person |
| deficiency disease | 营养缺乏病 | a disease caused by a lack of a nutrient in the diet |
| genetic disease | 遗传病 | a disease caused by a fault in the DNA |
| lifestyle disease | 生活方式病 | a disease made more likely by the way we live, e.g. smoking |
| autoimmune disease | 自身免疫病 | a disease in which the body's own white blood cells attack it |
| symptom | 症状 | an obvious effect of a disease, e.g. a rash |
| hygiene | 卫生 | keeping things clean so pathogens do not spread |
| immunisation | 免疫接种 | giving a vaccine to protect against a disease |

**Timeline**

| Phase | Min | Teacher does | Students do | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do Now | 4 | Shows the 1840s and today median ages of death. Takes reasons for the change. | Answer the three questions in their books. | Slide 2 |
| Today | 1 | Reads the two goals and points to the key words. | Listen; find the key words box on the worksheet. | Slide 3, worksheet header |
| Hook | 3 | Tells the Broad Street cholera story (1854). Asks how water could make people ill. | Pair talk, then share. | Slide 4 |
| Learn | 10 | Teaches why we live longer, the five types of disease, pathogens and symptoms. Runs the quick check. | Answer the quick check on whiteboards. | Slides 5 to 8 |
| Do | 14 | Sets the worksheet. Circulates: checks the disease type match and the communicable table. | Core, then Stretch, then Challenge. | Slide 9, worksheet |
| Think and Reflect | 4 | Asks why most deaths in richer countries are now from non-communicable diseases. | Discuss in pairs, share with a frame. | Slide 10 |
| Exit ticket | 4 | Collects exit tickets; previews next lesson (immunity). | Complete the exit ticket alone. | Slide 11, worksheet strip |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** Match each type of disease to an example; sort diseases into communicable and non-communicable; complete a gap fill on hygiene, immunisation and symptoms; identify anaemia as a deficiency disease.
- **Stretch:** Define pathogen, hygiene and immunisation; explain why nerve damage from polio stops muscles working; explain what an estimate is.
- **Challenge:** Explain why deaths in richer countries are now mostly from non-communicable diseases; explain how medicines enter cells by diffusion.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- Non-infectious diseases such as heart disease can be 'caught' from someone. Only diseases caused by pathogens can spread from person to person.
- 'Germs' are one kind of thing. Pathogens come from several kingdoms: bacteria, fungi, protoctists, and viruses (which are not living organisms).
- A lifestyle factor means the person will certainly get the disease. A risk factor raises the chance; it does not make the disease certain. Treat these examples with care in class.

**Safety:** No practical hazards beyond normal classroom rules.

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. deficiency: scurvy; infectious: flu; lifestyle: lung cancer linked to smoking; genetic: sickle cell disease; autoimmune: type 1 diabetes.
2. Communicable: cholera, chickenpox, malaria. Non-communicable: anaemia, cardiovascular disease, haemophilia.
3. microorganisms; sewage, hygiene; Immunisation; symptoms.
4. A, deficiency disease.

*Stretch*
1. Pathogen: a microorganism that causes disease. Hygiene: keeping things clean (e.g. drinking water separate from sewage) so pathogens do not spread. Immunisation: giving a vaccine so the body becomes protected (immune) against a particular disease.
2. Secure answer: muscles only contract when they receive electrical impulses along nerves from the brain and spinal cord. If the virus damages those nerves, the impulses cannot reach the leg muscles, so the muscles do not contract and the person cannot move their legs (paralysis).
3. Estimated means the number is an approximate calculation, not an exact count. It was not possible to count every case because many countries did not record every patient, some people were never diagnosed, and data from many areas could not be collected.

*Challenge*
1. Secure answer: in the 1840s many people died young from infectious diseases such as cholera and typhoid. Hygiene (clean water, sewers), immunisation (vaccines against polio, measles and so on) and antibiotics mean that far fewer people now die from infections. People therefore live much longer, and diseases that develop inside the body over a lifetime, such as heart disease and cancer, become the main causes of death. Lifestyle factors (smoking, unhealthy diet, too little exercise) increase the risk of these non-communicable diseases.
2. Secure answer: the medicine dissolves, so its particles are moving randomly all the time. There are more medicine particles in the blood than inside the cells, so there is a concentration gradient. More particles move from the blood (higher concentration) into the cells (lower concentration) than move the other way, so overall the medicine spreads into the cells. Diffusion does not need energy from the cell.

*My thinking:* Open answer. A good answer names the information needed: numbers of deaths from water-borne diseases before and after clean water supplies, numbers of deaths prevented by antibiotics and vaccines, and the dates when each change happened in different countries. Historians generally credit clean water and sewers with the biggest early fall in deaths.

*Exit ticket*
1. A microorganism that causes disease.
2. e.g. scurvy (caused by a lack of vitamin C), sickle cell disease (a faulty gene) or type 1 diabetes (the immune system attacks the pancreas): it is not caused by a pathogen, so it cannot be passed from person to person.
3. Any two of: bacteria, viruses, fungi, protoctists.

## Lesson 2: Immunity and vaccination | 免疫与疫苗接种

Student Book pp. 39 to 41 (9Ca Diseases (Immunity); 9Ca Veterinary science (STEM)) | Workbook pp. 29 to 30 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** White blood cells destroy pathogens, and memory cells mean the second attack by the same pathogen is beaten before you feel ill: that is immunity, and vaccines give it without the disease.

**Learning goals**
- I can describe how phagocytes and lymphocytes destroy microorganisms in the body.
- I can explain why you only get many infectious diseases once, and how vaccination protects people and communities.

**Key words**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| white blood cell | 白细胞 | a blood cell that fights pathogens |
| phagocyte | 吞噬细胞 | a white blood cell that surrounds, ingests and digests microorganisms |
| lymphocyte | 淋巴细胞 | a white blood cell that makes antibodies |
| antibody | 抗体 | a protein that sticks to one type of pathogen |
| specific | 特异性的 | fits only one type of pathogen |
| memory cell | 记忆细胞 | a lymphocyte that stays in the body ready to make antibodies quickly |
| immune | 免疫的 | protected against a disease, so you do not become ill with it |
| vaccine | 疫苗 | a substance containing parts of a pathogen, given to make a person immune |
| virus | 病毒 | a very small pathogen that can only copy itself inside a cell |
| dose | 剂量 | the amount of a medicine given at one time |
| half-life | 半衰期 | the time for the amount of a medicine in the body to fall by half |

**Timeline**

| Phase | Min | Teacher does | Students do | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Do | 14 | Sets the worksheet. Circulates: checks the gap fill and the true or false table; supports the half-life calculation in Challenge. | Core, then Stretch, then Challenge. | Slide 10, worksheet |
| Think and Reflect | 4 | Community protection: why are the unvaccinated 5% also protected? | Discuss in pairs, share with a frame. | Slide 11 |
| Exit ticket | 4 | Collects exit tickets; previews next lesson (nerves and hormones). | Complete the exit ticket alone. | Slide 12, worksheet strip |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** Gap fill on phagocytes, lymphocytes and antibodies; match the key words; true or false on antibodies, vaccines and memory cells.
- **Stretch:** Explain why having had measles does not protect against chickenpox; explain how a vaccine protects a child; explain why viruses are not living organisms.
- **Challenge:** Define dose and half-life and calculate when a ball python needs its next antibiotic dose; explain how vaccinating most of a community protects those who cannot be vaccinated.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- Antibodies kill all pathogens in the same way. Each antibody is specific: it fits the surface of one type of pathogen, so immunity to measles gives no protection against chickenpox.
- A vaccine gives you a mild case of the disease. A vaccine contains parts of a pathogen's surface (or a dead or weakened form) that cannot cause the disease, but still make lymphocytes produce antibodies and memory cells.
- Antibiotics treat any infection. Antibiotics kill bacteria; they do nothing to viruses (this lesson's Think task and Lesson 4).

**Safety:** No practical hazards beyond normal classroom rules.

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. pathogen; Phagocytes, lymphocytes; antibodies; pathogen (or microorganisms), stick; ingested.
2. phagocyte: ingests and digests microorganisms; lymphocyte: makes antibodies; antibody: protein that sticks to one type of pathogen; memory cell: stays ready to make antibodies quickly; vaccine: contains parts of a pathogen and makes a person immune.
3. True; False; False; True; True.

*Stretch*
1. Secure answer: antibodies are specific. The measles virus and the chickenpox virus have different surfaces, so the antibodies and memory cells made against measles do not fit the chickenpox virus. Lymphocytes would have to make a new antibody from the start, so the child can still become ill with chickenpox.
2. Secure answer: the vaccine contains parts of the surface coating of the measles virus (or a weakened form) that cannot cause the disease. Lymphocytes make antibodies that fit the measles virus, and memory cells are formed. If the real virus later infects the child, the memory cells make large amounts of the right antibody very quickly, so the virus is destroyed before the child becomes ill.
3. Viruses cannot carry out life processes on their own: they do not respire, grow, feed or move, and they can only copy themselves (reproduce) inside a living cell.

*Challenge*
1. a) The amount of medicine given at one time (here, in mg of antibiotic for each kg of the animal's mass). b) 10 mg/kg falls to 5 mg/kg after one half-life (64 hours) and to 2.5 mg/kg after two half-lives (128 hours). 128 hours = 5.3 days, so a second dose is needed by day 5 (accept 5 to 5.3 days). c) 15 mg/kg x 15 kg = 225 mg.
2. Secure answer: a pathogen spreads from one person to the next. Vaccinated people are immune, so they do not become ill and do not pass the pathogen on. When most people are vaccinated, the chain of infection is broken and the pathogen rarely reaches an unvaccinated person, so babies and ill people are protected as well. This only works if a high percentage of the community is vaccinated.

*My thinking:* Open answer. Good answers suggest that the flu virus changes its surface coating from year to year, so last year's antibodies and memory cells no longer fit and a new vaccine is needed; the polio virus does not change in this way.

*Exit ticket*
1. Phagocytes ingest (surround and digest) microorganisms; lymphocytes make antibodies.
2. After the first infection, memory cells remain and make the specific antibodies very quickly and in large numbers the next time, so the virus is destroyed before you become ill (you are immune).
3. Parts of the pathogen's surface coating (or a dead or weakened pathogen); these cannot cause the disease but lymphocytes still make antibodies and memory cells against them.

## Lesson 3: Control systems: nerves and hormones | 控制系统：神经与激素

Student Book pp. 42 to 43 (9Cb Control systems) | Workbook pp. 31 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** The body has two control systems: the nervous system sends fast electrical impulses along nerves, and the hormonal system sends slower chemical messengers in the blood.

**Learning goals**
- I can describe how the nervous system detects a stimulus and produces a response.
- I can describe how hormones affect the body and compare the nervous and hormonal systems.

**Key words**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| nervous system | 神经系统 | the organ system that senses changes and makes the body respond rapidly |
| central nervous system | 中枢神经系统 | the brain and spinal cord (CNS) |
| nerve | 神经 | an organ that carries impulses between the CNS and the rest of the body |
| stimulus | 刺激 | a change that can be detected, e.g. light, sound, touch |
| receptor | 感受器 | a cell in a sense organ that detects a stimulus |
| impulse | 神经冲动 | an electrical signal that travels along a nerve |
| effector | 效应器 | a muscle or gland that carries out a response |
| hormone | 激素 | a chemical messenger made in a gland and carried in the blood |
| gland | 腺体 | an organ that makes and releases a substance, e.g. a hormone |
| target organ | 靶器官 | an organ that responds to a particular hormone |
| insulin | 胰岛素 | a hormone from the pancreas that makes cells take up glucose from the blood |

**Timeline**

| Phase | Min | Teacher does | Students do | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do Now | 4 | Hot pan scenario: which organ detects, which moves, which connects. | Answer the three questions in their books. | Slide 2 |
| Today | 1 | Reads the two goals and points to the key words. | Listen; find the key words box on the worksheet. | Slide 3, worksheet header |
| Hook | 3 | Fast messages and slow changes: why two kinds of messenger? | Pair talk: one fast response, one slow change. | Slide 4 |
| Learn | 9 | Teaches the nervous pathway, the pen example, hormones and glands, and the comparison table. Runs the quick check. | Answer the quick check on whiteboards. | Slides 5 to 9 |
| Do | 15 | Runs the ruler-drop reaction time test (7 minutes), then the worksheet (8 minutes). Circulates: checks the pathway ordering. | Measure reaction time in pairs; record in the Core table; then Core, Stretch, Challenge. | Slide 10, worksheet |
| Think and Reflect | 4 | Treading on something sharp: which system is better? Takes answers with frames. | Discuss in pairs, share with a frame. | Slide 11 |
| Exit ticket | 4 | Collects exit tickets; previews next lesson (treating diseases). | Complete the exit ticket alone. | Slide 12, worksheet strip |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** Match sense organs to stimuli; gap fill on hormones; record and average reaction time results; put the pen pick-up pathway in order.
- **Stretch:** Name the organ that makes insulin and its effect; complete a comparison table of the two systems; explain how adrenaline prepares the body for danger.
- **Challenge:** Decide which system responds best to treading on something sharp; suggest which glands were affected in the tallest and shortest men.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- Nerves and hormones are the same kind of message. Nerves carry electrical impulses along fixed paths; hormones are chemicals carried everywhere by the blood and act only on their target organs.
- Hormones are only about puberty. Hormones control growth, heart rate, blood glucose and the rate of many body processes, all through life.
- The response to a stimulus happens in the sense organ. The receptor detects the stimulus; the brain or spinal cord processes it; a separate effector (muscle or gland) produces the response.

**Safety:** Reaction time test: students stay seated; rulers are dropped between the fingers, not thrown; keep feet clear of falling rulers.

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. eye: light; ear: sound; skin: temperature and touch; tongue: chemicals in food; nose: chemicals in the air.
2. hormones; glands; blood; target.
3. a) Student results; a typical mean is 10 to 20 cm. b) The nervous system, because the response had to be very fast (a fraction of a second) and only one effector (the hand muscles) was needed.
4. Order: the pen touches the fingers (1); touch receptors detect the pen (2); impulses travel along a nerve to the spinal cord and brain (3); the brain processes the information (4); impulses travel along a nerve to the muscles in the arm (5); the muscles contract and lift the hand (6).

*Stretch*
1. The pancreas. Insulin makes muscle cells (and liver cells) take up glucose from the blood, so the blood glucose level falls.
2. Nervous: electrical impulses along nerves; short, rapid response; acts only on the effector connected to the nerve. Hormonal: chemical messengers (hormones) in the blood; longer, slower response; acts on many target organs at the same time.
3. Secure answer: adrenaline is carried in the blood to many target organs, including the heart and lungs. It increases heart rate and breathing rate, so more oxygen and glucose reach the muscles for respiration, releasing more energy. The body is ready to run away or fight.

*Challenge*
1. Secure answer: the nervous system is better. Receptors in the skin of the foot detect the sharp object and produce impulses that travel along a nerve to the spinal cord and brain (the CNS). The CNS sends impulses along another nerve to the leg muscles (the effector), which contract and lift the foot. This takes a fraction of a second; a hormone released into the blood would be far too slow and would act on many organs, not just the leg.
2. Secure answer: the pituitary gland makes growth hormone, which controls the normal growth of children, so it is likely that Sultan Kosen's pituitary produced too much growth hormone and Chandra Dangi's produced too little. The thyroid gland (thyroxine controls the rate of many body processes) may also have been involved. Accept either gland with a clear reason.

## Lesson 7: In and out: surface area, diffusion and osmosis | 进与出：表面积、扩散与渗透

Student Book pp. 50 to 52 (9Ce In and out; 9Ce Combatting pandemics) | Workbook pp. 36 to 38 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** Substances get into and out of organisms by diffusion and osmosis, and that only works fast enough when the surface area is large compared with the volume.

**Learning goals**

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- Particles stop moving when diffusion is finished. Particles keep moving randomly; once they are evenly spread there is no overall (net) movement.
- Osmosis is the movement of the sugar or salt. Osmosis is the movement of the solvent (water) through the membrane; the large sugar molecules cannot pass.
- Bigger organisms have a bigger surface area : volume ratio. The surface area grows more slowly than the volume, so the ratio gets smaller as things get bigger.
