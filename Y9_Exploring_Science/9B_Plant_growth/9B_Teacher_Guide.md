## Lesson 1: Reactions in plants | 植物中的化学反应

Student Book pp. 21 to 23 (9Ba On a farm; 9Ba Reactions in plants) | Workbook pp. 15 to 16 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** Plants make their own food by photosynthesis and release the energy stored in it by aerobic respiration; a farmer's harvest depends on both.

**Learning goals**
- I can write the word and symbol equations for photosynthesis and aerobic respiration, and say where and when each happens in a plant.
- I can explain how limiting factors slow photosynthesis and why roots need oxygen.

**Key words**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| photosynthesis | 光合作用 | the process plants use to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, using light |
| aerobic respiration | 有氧呼吸 | the reaction in every living cell that releases energy from glucose using oxygen |
| chlorophyll | 叶绿素 | the green substance that captures energy from light |
| chloroplast | 叶绿体 | the part of a plant cell where photosynthesis happens |
| glucose | 葡萄糖 | the sugar made in photosynthesis |
| reactant | 反应物 | a substance used up in a chemical reaction |
| product | 产物 | a substance made in a chemical reaction |
| limiting factor | 限制因素 | a variable that slows down the rate of photosynthesis |
| phloem | 韧皮部 | tubes made of living cells that carry sugar solution around a plant |
| yield | 产量 | the amount of useful product a farmer gets from a crop |

**Timeline**

| Exit ticket | 4 | Collects exit tickets; summary; sets Workbook pages 15 to 16. | Complete the exit ticket alone. | Slides 13 to 14, worksheet strip |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** Complete both word equations; sort statements about photosynthesis and respiration; match key words; calculate the mass changes in van Helmont's experiment.
- **Stretch:** Read the pondweed gas graph; explain three limiting factors; explain why flooding harms crops.
- **Challenge:** Draw a flow chart for energy from the Sun to a plant; evaluate the claim that the willow's extra mass came from water.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- Plants take in food from the soil. Water and mineral salts are not food: plants are producers that make their own food (glucose) by photosynthesis.
- Light is a reactant that is turned into food. Light supplies the energy; the substances that react are carbon dioxide and water.
- Plants photosynthesise instead of respiring. Every living plant cell respires all the time; photosynthesis only happens in the light in cells with chloroplasts.

**Safety:** No practical hazards beyond normal classroom rules.

**Equipment**
- Worksheets (standard and Chinese key words versions)
- Mini whiteboards for the quick check (optional)
- A pot plant and a potted plant kept in a dark cupboard for a week, to compare (optional demonstration)

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water makes glucose + oxygen. Aerobic respiration: glucose + oxygen makes carbon dioxide + water (+ energy released).
2. Photosynthesis: only in light; takes in carbon dioxide; happens in chloroplasts. Respiration: all the time; releases energy from glucose; in every living cell.
3. chlorophyll: the green substance that captures energy from light; chloroplast: the part of a plant cell where photosynthesis happens; limiting factor: a variable that slows the rate; phloem: tubes of living cells carrying sugar solution; glucose: the sugar made in photosynthesis.
4. a) 90.72 - 90.66 = 0.06 kg lost. b) 76.74 - 2.27 = 74.47 kg gained. c) The tree gained over 74 kg but the soil lost only 0.06 kg, so the extra mass of the tree could not have come from the soil; the plant did not 'eat' soil.

*Stretch*
1. a) X is oxygen: it rises during daylight because the plant makes oxygen by photosynthesis faster than it uses it. b) After dark there is no light, so photosynthesis stops; the plant keeps respiring, which uses up oxygen, so the concentration falls.
2. Secure answer: light: if the lamp is dim, chlorophyll captures less energy, so the rate cannot rise. Carbon dioxide: if the water has little dissolved carbon dioxide, there is not enough reactant. Temperature: if the water is cold the reactions are slow. (Also accept water shortage.) Each factor can be the one that limits the rate, so adding more of the others does not help.
3. Secure answer: glucose is made in the leaves and carried to the roots as sugar solution in the phloem. Oxygen comes from air spaces in the soil. When a field floods, water fills the air spaces, so the roots get no oxygen, cannot respire to release energy, and die; without roots the crop dies.

*Challenge*
1. Light from the Sun -> energy captured by chlorophyll in chloroplasts -> energy used to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water (photosynthesis) -> energy stored in glucose (and in starch made from glucose).
2. Secure answer: van Helmont was right that the mass did not come from the soil, but wrong that it was all water. The word equation shows that glucose is made from carbon dioxide and water, so much of the carbon in the tree came from carbon dioxide in the air. He could not have known this because carbon dioxide had not been discovered. A valid conclusion would say the mass came from water and something else that was not the soil.

*My thinking:* Yes: plants respire all the time and take in oxygen for aerobic respiration. At night, when there is no photosynthesis, a plant takes in more oxygen than it gives out.

*Exit ticket*
1. carbon dioxide + water makes glucose + oxygen
2. In the chloroplasts.
3. Photosynthesis needs light (energy captured by chlorophyll), and in a field light is only available in the day.

## Lesson 2: Plant adaptations: roots, stems and leaves | 植物的适应：根、茎和叶

Student Book pp. 24 to 25 (9Bb Plant adaptations (mineral salts, water, light)) | Workbook pp. 17 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** Roots, stems and leaves are adapted to supply every cell with water, mineral salts, light and carbon dioxide.

**Learning goals**
- I can describe what plants need from the soil and the air, and how roots, root hair cells and xylem are adapted to supply water and mineral salts.
- I can explain how a leaf and its palisade cells are adapted for photosynthesis.

**Key words**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| mineral salts | 矿物盐 | substances dissolved in soil water that plants need to stay healthy, e.g. nitrates |
| nitrate | 硝酸盐 | a mineral salt that supplies nitrogen for making proteins |
| magnesium | 镁 | an element plants need to make chlorophyll |
| potassium | 钾 | an element root cells need to absorb water properly |
| root hair cell | 根毛细胞 | a root cell with a long extension that gives a large surface area for absorbing water |
| xylem | 木质部 | hollow tubes made from dead cells that carry water and mineral salts up the plant |
| wilt | 枯萎 | to droop because the cells have too little water |
| palisade cell | 栅栏细胞 | a leaf cell near the upper surface that is packed with chloroplasts |
| epidermis | 表皮 | the outer layer of cells of a leaf |
| cuticle | 角质层 | the waxy layer on a leaf that stops it losing too much water |
| surface area | 表面积 | the total area of the outside of something |

**Timeline**

| Phase | Min | Teacher does | Students do | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Hook | 3 | Venus flytrap: why does a plant eat flies? Collects ideas. | Pair talk, share. | Slide 4 |

| Do | 14 | Sets the worksheet. Optional: pairs trace a leaf on squared paper and estimate its surface area. | Core, then Stretch, then Challenge. | Slide 11, worksheet |
| Think and Reflect | 4 | Wilting and recovery: takes answers with the frames. | Pair discussion, share with a frame. | Slide 12 |
| Exit ticket | 4 | Collects exit tickets; summary; sets Workbook page 17. | Complete the exit ticket alone. | Slides 13 to 14, worksheet strip |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** Complete the table of what plants need and where it comes from; match leaf parts to functions; gap fill on the route of water.
- **Stretch:** Explain the adaptations of xylem and root hair cells; explain wilting and recovery; explain why a magnesium shortage slows growth.
- **Challenge:** Explain the Venus flytrap's source of mineral salts; describe how a leaf gets palisade cells everything they need.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- Plants get their food from the soil. Plants get water and mineral salts from the soil, but they make their food (glucose) by photosynthesis in the leaves.
- Mineral salts are food. They are not an energy source: nitrates are used to build proteins and magnesium to build chlorophyll.
- Students do not link the shape of a structure to its job. Every adaptation here (root hairs, hollow xylem, thin broad leaves, chloroplast-packed palisade cells) is a shape that fits a function.

**Safety:** No practical hazards beyond normal classroom rules.

**Equipment**

- Optional: a leaf, squared paper and a ruler per pair to estimate leaf surface area
- Optional: a wilted and a watered pot plant to compare

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. oxygen: air (and soil air for roots), respiration. carbon dioxide: air, photosynthesis. nitrate salts: soil water, making proteins. magnesium salts: soil water, making chlorophyll. potassium salts: soil water, absorbing water (roots). water: soil water, photosynthesis and keeping cells firm (also cooling).
2. palisade cells: contain many chloroplasts near the top; phloem: carries dissolved sugars from the leaf; xylem: carries water and mineral salts from the roots; stomata: allow gases to enter and leave; cuticle: stops the leaf losing too much water.
3. root hair; surface area; xylem; dead; wilts.

*Stretch*
1. a) Xylem cells die and become hollow, and their end walls break down, so chains of them form long continuous tubes that carry water and mineral salts up the plant. b) Root hair cells have a long thin extension, which gives a large surface area, so they absorb water (with dissolved mineral salts) from the soil quickly.
2. Secure answer: magnesium is needed to make chlorophyll. With less chlorophyll (yellow leaves) the plant captures less energy from light, so photosynthesis is slower, less glucose is made, and there is less glucose for respiration and for building new cells, so growth is slow.
3. Any three: water is a reactant in photosynthesis; it keeps cells filled so leaves stay firm and the plant does not wilt; it keeps the leaves cool (evaporation); it carries dissolved mineral salts from the soil; it dissolves sugars so they can be moved in the phloem.

*Challenge*
1. a) Shutting the trap uses energy; waiting for a second touch means it does not shut on raindrops or dust, only on an insect that is moving. b) The insects supply mineral salts, mainly nitrates for making proteins and potassium. The plant can make glucose by photosynthesis, but without nitrates it cannot make proteins (including enzymes) to grow and repair itself, and without potassium its roots cannot absorb water properly, so it could die.
2. Secure answer: light: the leaf is broad (large surface area) and thin, the upper epidermis is clear, and palisade cells are just under it where light is strongest. Carbon dioxide: stomata let it diffuse in, the spongy layer has air spaces so it reaches every cell, and the leaf is thin so the distance is short. Water: xylem in the veins brings water from the roots to the cells, and the waxy cuticle stops too much being lost.

*My thinking:* Yes (hydroponics). The plants still need water and dissolved mineral salts (from the solution), carbon dioxide and oxygen (from the air, and oxygen bubbled into the water for the roots) and light (sunlight or lamps). The soil only ever supplied water, mineral salts and support.

*Exit ticket*
1. Nitrates.
2. They have a long thin extension, which gives a large surface area, so water is absorbed quickly.
3. Photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts, and palisade cells are near the upper surface where there is most light.

## Lesson 3: Gas exchange in leaves | 叶片的气体交换

Student Book pp. 25 (9Bb Plant adaptations (carbon dioxide, stomata and gas exchange)) | Workbook pp. 18 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** Gases enter and leave a leaf by diffusion through stomata, which guard cells open in the light and close at night.

**Learning goals**
- I can describe how stomata and guard cells control gas exchange in a leaf.
- I can explain how temperature and concentration difference affect diffusion, and why a plant loses more water on a hot day.

**Key words**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| stomata | 气孔 | tiny holes in a leaf that let gases in and out (one is a stoma) |
| guard cell | 保卫细胞 | one of the pair of cells that opens and closes a stoma |
| diffusion | 扩散 | the overall movement of particles from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated |
| concentration | 浓度 | how many particles of a substance there are in a certain space |
| gas exchange | 气体交换 | the swapping of gases between a leaf and the air |
| water vapour | 水蒸气 | water in the form of a gas |
| evaporation | 蒸发 | a liquid turning into a gas |
| wilt | 枯萎 | to droop because the cells have too little water |
| epidermis | 表皮 | the outer layer of cells of a leaf |

**Timeline**

| Phase | Min | Teacher does | Students do | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do Now | 4 | Shows the stoma micrograph and the three questions. | Answer in their books. | Slide 2 |
| Today | 1 | Reads the two goals; points to the key words. | Find the key words box on the worksheet. | Slide 3, worksheet header |
| Hook | 3 | Two identical plants, one in a hot place has wilted. Why? Collects ideas, no answer yet. | Pair talk, share. | Slide 4 |
| Learn | 8 | Teaches stomata and guard cells, diffusion, gas exchange in the leaf, water loss. Quick check. | Answer the quick check. | Slides 5 to 9 |
| Do | 16 | Safety slide, then the nail varnish peel practical. Circulates: checks the peel is thin and the slide is in focus. Worksheet Core while varnish dries. | Make and view the epidermis peel; draw a stoma; count stomata; then Core, Stretch, Challenge. | Slides 10 to 12, worksheet |
| Think and Reflect | 4 | Back to the hook: why did the hot plant wilt? Takes answers with the frames. | Pair discussion, share with a frame. | Slide 13 |
| Exit ticket | 4 | Collects exit tickets; summary; sets Workbook page 18. | Complete the exit ticket alone. | Slides 14 to 15, worksheet strip |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** Label and describe the open and closed stoma; gap fill on diffusion into the leaf; true or false on diffusion; record the stomata count.
- **Stretch:** Explain why stomata open in the light and close at night; choose the correct words to explain wilting on a hot day; explain the effect of wind.
- **Challenge:** Suggest why most stomata are on the lower surface; estimate the number of stomata on a leaf from a count.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- Gases are 'sucked in' or 'breathed in' by the leaf. Particles move by themselves; diffusion is the overall movement down a concentration gradient and needs no pulling.
- Particles only move in one direction. Particles move randomly in every direction; the net movement is from high to low concentration.
- Plants only give out oxygen. In daylight more oxygen leaves than enters, but carbon dioxide and water vapour also pass through the stomata, and at night a plant takes in oxygen.

**Safety:** Clear nail varnish: use in a ventilated room, keep away from flames, do not breathe the fumes, wash hands afterwards. Microscopes: carry with two hands; do not touch the lamp. Sticky tape and leaves are low hazard.

**Equipment**
- Per pair: a leaf with a smooth underside (geranium, spider plant, busy lizzie or similar), clear nail varnish, clear sticky tape, scissors, microscope slide, microscope
- Prepared slide of a leaf epidermis as a back-up
- Worksheets (standard and Chinese key words versions)

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. a) To open and close the stoma. b) Carbon dioxide and oxygen (also water vapour). c) The closed one, because stomata shut at night when there is no light for photosynthesis, which saves water.
2. photosynthesis; concentration; fall; rapidly.
3. False (it is the other way round); True; True; False (they keep moving, but there is no overall change); True.
4. a) A drawing of two curved (sausage-shaped) guard cells with the pore between them, both labelled. b) Three counts and a mean; accept any sensible values (typically 5 to 40 depending on the leaf and magnification).

*Stretch*
1. Secure answer: in the light the plant is photosynthesising and needs carbon dioxide, so open stomata let it diffuse in (advantage: faster photosynthesis). At night there is no photosynthesis so no carbon dioxide is needed; shutting the stomata stops water vapour diffusing out (advantage: the plant saves water and does not wilt).
2. increases; increase, increase; a decrease; more.
3. Secure answer: wind blows away the air next to the leaf that contains the oxygen that has just diffused out, so the concentration of oxygen outside the leaf stays low. The difference in concentration between the inside and the outside of the leaf stays large, and a bigger concentration difference means faster diffusion.

*Challenge*
1. e.g. the lower surface is shaded and cooler, so less water evaporates through the stomata; the upper surface gets the direct sunlight and can keep a thick waxy cuticle to stop water loss; rain and dust do not block the holes underneath. Any two with reasons.
2. 32 stomata per 0.5 mm² = 64 per mm². 64 x 4000 = 256 000 stomata (about 260 000). Accept correct working with a different mean from their own count.

*My thinking:* A cactus lives where the day is very hot and dry; opening stomata at night, when it is cooler, means much less water is lost by evaporation and diffusion. The problem: no carbon dioxide can enter during the day when there is light, so the cactus has to store the carbon dioxide it takes in at night and photosynthesis is slower.

*Exit ticket*
1. They open and close the stomata.
2. Carbon dioxide and oxygen (or water vapour).
3. At a higher temperature particles move faster, so water evaporates faster inside the leaf and water vapour diffuses out through the stomata faster.

*Stretch*

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. 1 Nutrients dissolve in soil water; 2 Soil water with extra nutrients leaks into a stream or river; 3 Extra nutrients cause rapid growth of algae and water plants; 4 Algae and plants at the surface block the light, so deeper plants die; 5 Decomposers break down the dead plants, using oxygen; 6 Fish die because there is too little oxygen.
2. a) wheat or hazel tree. b) sparrows and dormice (accept sparrows only if the student reads dormice as eating hazel). c) aphids: decreases; caterpillars: decreases; sparrows: decreases (less food); sparrowhawks: decreases (fewer sparrows). Accept 'no change at first' for sparrowhawks with a reason.
3. photosynthesis: removes carbon dioxide from the air; respiration: releases carbon dioxide from living cells; combustion: releases carbon dioxide when fuels or wood burn; decomposers feeding: releases carbon dioxide from dead plants and animals; animals feeding: passes carbon compounds from plants to animals.

*Stretch*
1. Secure answer: the bacteria are decomposers that break down dead plants and algae. Decomposers respire, which uses up the oxygen dissolved in the water. Fish need this oxygen for their own respiration, so with less oxygen the fish cannot release enough energy and they die.
2. Secure answer: nitrates supply nitrogen for making amino acids and proteins, which plants and algae need to build new cells and enzymes. Normally the amount of nitrate in a lake limits growth; with extra nitrates this limiting factor is removed, so the algae and water plants can grow and reproduce much faster.
3. Secure answer: 1) Trees remove carbon dioxide from the air by photosynthesis; fewer trees means less carbon dioxide is removed, so it builds up. 2) When the cut trees are burnt, combustion releases the carbon stored in the wood as carbon dioxide; if they are left to rot, decomposers release it by respiration.

*Challenge*
1. Secure answer: all the potato plants were the same variety, so they were genetically identical. When a disease (potato blight) arrived, every plant was equally unable to resist it, so the whole crop died in field after field. With such low biodiversity (one variety, one crop) there was nothing else to eat, and the harvest could not recover quickly, so people starved.
2. a) The insecticide washed into the river and was taken in by tiny organisms and plants; small fish ate many of them, bigger fish ate many small fish, and the birds ate many big fish. Because the insecticide is persistent it is not broken down, so at each step the amount in each animal is larger: it bioaccumulates, and the top predator ends up with the most. b) Rapeseed flowers and is visited by bees, so the insecticide in its nectar and pollen poisons the bees; wheat is pollinated by the wind, so bees do not feed on it.

*My thinking:* 1) The chemical reaction itself releases carbon dioxide: calcium carbonate turns into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. 2) Heating the limestone needs a lot of energy, usually from burning fossil fuels, and combustion releases more carbon dioxide.

*Exit ticket*
1. A very rich supply of nutrients (e.g. from fertiliser) in water, which causes rapid growth of algae and plants and ends with fish dying from a lack of oxygen.
2. Any two of: respiration, combustion (burning), decomposition by decomposers.
3. All the plants are identical, so if one gets a disease they all do and the whole crop can be wiped out.

## Lesson 7: Bias, validity and organic farming | 偏差、有效性与有机农业

Student Book pp. 34 to 36 (9Be Bias and validity (WS); 9Be Organic farming) | Workbook pp. 25 to 26 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** Before we believe a graph or an argument about farming we check it for bias and ask whether the conclusion is valid.

**Learning goals**
- I can identify bias in graphs and arguments, explain where accidental bias comes from, and say whether a conclusion is valid.
- I can compare organic and intensive farming and explain biological control.

**Key words**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| bias | 偏差 | a shift away from the true meaning or value, on purpose or by mistake |
| balanced | 客观全面的 | considering the points for and against something equally |
| systematic error | 系统误差 | an error that shifts every reading by the same amount, e.g. a wrongly printed scale |
| random error | 随机误差 | an error that changes from one reading to the next, e.g. stopping a clock late once |
| sample | 样本 | a small part of a whole that is measured to estimate the whole |
| valid | 有效的 | does what it is supposed to do; a valid conclusion uses only the results |
| conclusion | 结论 | what the results of an investigation show |
| organic farming | 有机农业 | farming with natural fertilisers, very few pesticides and crop rotation |
| intensive farming | 集约农业 | farming with artificial fertilisers, pesticides and large fields to get the biggest yield |
| biological control | 生物防治 | using living organisms to reduce the number of pests |
| crop rotation | 轮作 | growing different crops in a field in different years |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** Choose and justify the valid conclusion for the fertiliser data; match the kinds of error and bias to examples; sort statements into organic or intensive farming.
- **Stretch:** Explain why the German insect study conclusion may not be valid; explain how graph C is biased and who might draw it; explain which farm attracts more insect-eating birds.
- **Challenge:** Write a balanced paragraph about HT soybean; compare the yields of organic and intensive farms and explain why people still choose organic food.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- Bias is always deliberate. A thermometer with a wrong scale or a badly chosen sample biases results without anyone intending it.
- If a graph shows a pattern, the conclusion is valid. A valid conclusion uses all the results and only the results; a selected part of the data can show a pattern that is not there.
- Organic food is proven to be healthier. There is little evidence for this; the clearer differences are in biodiversity, land use and cost.

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. a) D. b) The results show growth rising from 2 to 6 drops and then falling at 8 and 10 drops; D describes the whole pattern, A ignores the fall, B mentions harm from too little fertiliser (not tested) and C is the opposite of the first three results. c) Any two of: same type of seedling, same amount of water, same light, same temperature, same type of soil and pot, same time (one week).
2. systematic error: a mass balance not set to zero; random error: the stop clock stopped late in one test; valid experiment: all other variables controlled; intentional bias: only data supporting a view are quoted.
3. Organic: natural fertilisers; small fields with hedges; crop rotation with clover. Intensive: many artificial pesticides; large fields for big machines; the biggest yield from each field.

*Stretch*
1. Secure answer, any two: the traps only catch insects flying at about one metre, so insects that live on the ground, in the soil or high in trees were never counted, and the sample does not represent all insects; the traps were in particular places, which may not represent the whole of Germany; a change in mass may be due to a few large species, not insects in general; the method or the places may have changed over 24 years.
2. a) It uses only four of the years (1997, 1999, 2001, 2003), leaving out the years when the numbers rose, so it shows a steady fall that the full data do not show. b) e.g. someone who wants to blame HT soybean or glyphosate for the fall in monarchs, such as a group campaigning against herbicides, or a company selling a rival product, because the selected data support their view.
3. Secure answer: the organic farm, because its small fields are surrounded by hedges and trees where birds can nest and shelter, and because it uses few pesticides, so there are many more insects for the birds to eat; the intensive farm's large open fields and insecticides mean fewer nesting places and less food.

*Challenge*
1. Secure answer: a paragraph that gives at least two advantages (easier weed control; fewer sprayings; glyphosate breaks down and is not persistent) and at least two disadvantages (expensive seed; glyphosate-resistant weeds become common so other herbicides are needed; HT soybean becoming a weed in other crops), treats them equally, and ends with a decision that depends on the farmer's situation (e.g. worth it on a large weedy farm for a few years, less so for a small farm).
2. a) The intensive farm, because artificial fertilisers and pesticides give a higher yield from every field, while the organic farm needs much more space to grow the same amount of food. b) People choose organic food because they think it is healthier, because they do not want artificial substances on their food, or because organic farms cause less environmental damage and have more biodiversity. The evidence that organic food is healthier is weak (there is little evidence); the evidence for higher biodiversity on organic farms is strong.

*My thinking:* e.g. who paid for the study and whether they sell organic food (bias); how many people were studied and how they were chosen (sampling); what 'healthier' was measured as and whether other differences between the groups were controlled (validity); whether all the results are reported or only the ones that fit; whether other scientists have checked the work.

*Exit ticket*
1. A shift away from the true meaning or value, either on purpose or by mistake.
2. e.g. a thermometer with a wrongly printed scale that always reads 1 °C too high; a mass balance not set to zero.
3. e.g. organic farms use natural fertilisers and very few pesticides; intensive farms use artificial fertilisers and many artificial pesticides (also: field size, biodiversity, yield, cost).
