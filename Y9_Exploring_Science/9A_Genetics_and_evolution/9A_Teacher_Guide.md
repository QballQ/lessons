## Lesson 1: Environmental variation | 环境变异

Student Book pp. 5 to 7 (9Aa Monsters and myth; 9Aa Environmental variation) | Workbook pp. 3 to 4 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** The surroundings of an organism (its environment) can change its characteristics, and this environmental variation can be continuous or discontinuous.

**Learning goals**
- I can describe abiotic and biotic environmental factors and give examples of environmental variation.
- I can tell continuous variation from discontinuous variation, and explain why environmental variation can make classification difficult.

**Key words**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| species | 物种 | a group of organisms that can breed together to produce offspring that can also breed |
| extinct | 灭绝的 | a species with no living members left |
| environment | 环境 | the surroundings of an organism |
| abiotic factor | 非生物因素 | a physical (non-living) part of the environment, e.g. temperature |
| biotic factor | 生物因素 | the activities of other organisms, e.g. predation, disease |
| characteristic | 特征 | a feature of an organism |
| variation | 变异 | the differences between the characteristics of organisms |
| environmental variation | 环境变异 | variation caused by an organism's environment |
| continuous variation | 连续变异 | variation that can have any value between two points |
| discontinuous variation | 不连续变异 | variation with only a limited set of possible values |
| classification | 分类 | sorting organisms into groups |

**Timeline**

| Phase | Min | Teacher does | Students do | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do Now | 4 | Shows the 'dragon bones'. Takes answers; asks what 'extinct' means. | Answer the three questions in their books. | Slide 2 |
| Today | 1 | Reads the two goals and points to the key words. | Listen; find the key words box on the worksheet. | Slide 3, worksheet header |
| Hook | 3 | Giant pumpkins: 'Why so big?' Collects ideas: light, water, warmth, mineral salts, no competition. | Pair talk, then share. | Slide 4 |
| Learn | 10 | Teaches environmental factors (abiotic, biotic), environmental variation, continuous and discontinuous, species and classification. Runs the quick check. | Answer the quick check on whiteboards or with fingers. | Slides 5 to 9 |
| Do | 14 | Sets the worksheet. Circulates: checks tally charts and bar charts (no gaps for grouped continuous data). | Core, then Stretch, then Challenge. | Slide 10, worksheet |
| Think and Reflect | 4 | Seashore plant puzzle: takes answers with the sentence frames. | Discuss in pairs, share with a frame. | Slide 11 |
| Exit ticket | 4 | Collects exit tickets; previews next lesson (inherited variation). | Complete the exit ticket alone. | Slide 12, worksheet strip |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** Match key words to meanings; sort abiotic and biotic factors; tally and plot the potato mass data; say why the variation is continuous.
- **Stretch:** Give examples of environmental variation with their causes; classify characteristics as continuous or discontinuous; explain why potatoes of one variety vary in mass.
- **Challenge:** Use the seashore plant evidence to explain how environmental variation can make identifying species difficult; evaluate the Flores Island hypotheses.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- 'Variation' only means big differences. Any difference in a characteristic, however small, is variation.
- Students often think all variation can be passed on to offspring. Environmental variation (a scar, a hairstyle, a wind-bent tree) is not inherited. Lesson 2 builds on this.
- Continuous means 'keeps happening'. Here it means the measurement can take any value in a range (height), unlike discontinuous (blood group).

**Safety:** No practical hazards beyond normal classroom rules.

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. species: a group of organisms that can breed together to produce offspring that can also breed; extinct: a species with no living members left; environment: the surroundings of an organism; abiotic factor: a non-living factor; biotic factor: the activities of other organisms.
2. Abiotic: temperature, amount of light, rainfall. Biotic: predation, competition for food, infectious disease.
3. a) 110 to 129: 4 (118, 129, 121, 127); 130 to 149: 7 (142, 137, 146, 149, 133, 140, 148); 150 to 169: 6 (165, 151, 158, 160, 155, 163); 170 to 189: 3 (174, 183, 171). Total 20. b) Bars touch (grouped continuous data), x-axis 'Mass range (g)', y-axis 'Number of potatoes', title. c) Continuous, because mass can take any value between the smallest and largest.

*Stretch*
1. e.g. a scar (caused by a sharp object or fire, a physical factor); a hairstyle or dyed hair (fashion, caused by other people); a suntan (sunlight); a broken bone that healed crooked (an accident).
2. Continuous: height, hand span, length of a leaf. Discontinuous: blood group, number of fingers, tongue rolling.
3. Secure answer: the potatoes grew in slightly different environments: different amounts of water, light, mineral salts and space, or were attacked by pests or disease. These environmental factors caused environmental variation in mass even with the same variety (the same genetic information).

*Challenge*
1. a) The plants from both areas are the same species: when the environment was the same they all looked the same, so the differences were environmental variation. b) In the wild, the environment (wind, salt, soil, light) changes how a plant looks, so two groups of the same species can look like different species and a scientist could classify them wrongly. Growing them in the same conditions, or breeding them together, shows whether they are one species.
2. e.g. test DNA from the bones and compare with modern humans; look for signs of iodine deficiency in the bones and teeth; find more skeletons to see whether all adults were small; compare bone and skull shape with our species. Reasoning: if the DNA matches ours and the bones show poor nutrition, the short height was environmental; if the DNA and bone structure are different, they were a separate species.

*My thinking:* Disagree: some variation is inherited (passed on from parents in genetic information), e.g. natural eye colour or blood group, which do not change with the environment. Accept any correct example. This leads into Lesson 2.

*Exit ticket*
1. Abiotic: e.g. temperature, light, oxygen in the water. Biotic: e.g. predation, competition, disease.
2. Environmental.
3. Continuous, because foot length can take any value between two points (any measurement, not a limited set).

## Lesson 2: Inherited variation | 遗传变异

Student Book pp. 8 to 9 (9Ab Inherited variation) | Workbook pp. 5 to 6 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** Offspring inherit a mixture of characteristics from two parents because two different gametes fuse at fertilisation; inherited variation can be continuous or discontinuous.

**Learning goals**
- I can give examples of inherited variation and explain how sexual reproduction causes it.
- I can sort inherited variation into continuous and discontinuous, and recognise a normal distribution.

**Key words**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| inherited variation | 遗传变异 | differences in characteristics that are passed on from parents to offspring |
| offspring | 后代 | the young produced by parents |
| genetic information | 遗传信息 | the instructions for inherited characteristics, stored in the nucleus |
| gene | 基因 | a section of DNA that carries the instructions for one characteristic |
| nucleus | 细胞核 | the part of a cell that contains the genetic information |
| gamete | 配子 | a sex cell (a sperm cell or an egg cell) |
| fertilisation | 受精 | when a male gamete and a female gamete fuse (join together) |
| zygote | 受精卵 | the fertilised egg cell |
| continuous variation | 连续变异 | variation that can have any value between two points |
| discontinuous variation | 不连续变异 | variation with only a limited set of possible values |
| normal distribution | 正态分布 | the bell shape you get when grouped continuous data are plotted |

**Timeline**

| Phase | Min | Teacher does | Students do | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do Now | 4 | Shows the litter of kittens; asks how they vary and which differences came from the parents. | Answer the three questions in their books. | Slide 2 |
| Today | 1 | Reads the goals; points to the key words box on the worksheet. | Listen; find the key words. | Slide 3 |
| Hook | 3 | Dragon fruit: three examples of inherited variation; which could a grower change? | Pair talk, then share. | Slide 4 |
| Learn | 10 | Teaches inherited variation, gametes and the zygote, continuous and discontinuous inherited variation, the normal distribution. Runs the quick check. | Follow the diagram; answer the quick check. | Slides 5 to 10 |
| Do | 13 | Runs the class survey (tongue rolling, ear lobes, hand span) and sets the worksheet. | Collect class data; Core, then Stretch, then Challenge. | Slide 11, worksheet |
| Think and Reflect | 4 | Tall parents, short child: takes explanations with the frames. | Pair discussion, share with a frame. | Slide 12 |
| Exit ticket | 3 | Collects exit tickets. | Complete the exit ticket alone. | Slide 13 |
| Summary | 2 | Recaps; previews probability (Lesson 3). | Tick the goals they met. | Slide 14 |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** Gap fill on gametes, fertilisation and the zygote; sort characteristics by cause (genes, environment, both); record the class survey and say whether it is continuous or discontinuous.
- **Stretch:** Read the blood group bar chart (percentages and a calculation); explain foot length versus shoe size; explain why brothers and sisters differ.
- **Challenge:** A tomato characteristic caused by both genes and environment; interpret the height histogram and the normal distribution.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- Offspring inherit all their features from one parent (often the mother). Both gametes carry genetic information, so offspring have some characteristics of each parent.
- Features caused by the environment or lifestyle can be inherited. Only characteristics controlled by genetic information are passed on; a scar or a learned language is not.
- Continuous variation is only caused by genes. Height and skin colour are inherited but are also affected by diet and sunlight, so they are caused by both.

**Safety:** No practical hazards beyond normal classroom rules. Keep the class survey to tongue rolling, ear lobes and hand span; avoid body mass or anything a student may find sensitive.

**Slides and speaker notes**

| Slide | Type | Title | Speaker notes |

Ask: why can two children of the same parents have different eye colours? Because each gamete carries a different selection of the parent's genetic information. |
| 7 | compare | Inherited variation can be continuous or discontinuous | Shoe size is a good trap: feet show continuous variation, but shoes only come in certain sizes, so shoe size is discontinuous. Students meet this on the worksheet. |

Write the hand spans on the board as students call them out, so the Stretch plot has real class data. If the class is small, use the sample data on the worksheet. |
| 12 | think | Tall parents, short child? | Expected: each gamete carries a different selection of the parents' genetic information, so the child may inherit instructions for shorter height; height is also affected by the environment (diet, illness, exercise). Praise answers that use both ideas. |
| 13 | exit | Exit ticket | Answers: in the nucleus (in genes); a zygote; discontinuous and inherited. Students who get Q3 wrong need the 'limited set of values' idea again in Lesson 3. |
| 14 | summary | What we learned | Homework: Workbook pages 5 and 6 (9Ab Inherited variation and More variation). |

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. genetic; inherited; fuse; fertilisation; zygote; parents; characteristics; offspring.
2. Speaking more than one language: environment. Length of hair: environment. Human height: both. Natural eye colour: genes. Blood group: genes. A scar: environment.
3. a) Class totals. b) Discontinuous, because there are only two possible values (can roll or cannot roll); nothing in between.

*Stretch*
1. a) 24%. b) 7% of 300 = 0.07 x 300 = 21 people. c) discontinuous and inherited.
2. Foot length can take any value between the shortest and longest foot (for example 24.3 cm), so it is continuous. Shoes are only made in certain sizes (for example 37, 38, 39), so shoe size can only take a limited set of values: discontinuous.
3. Secure answer: each parent makes gametes that carry slightly different selections of their genetic information; no two gametes are identical. Each child comes from a different sperm cell and egg cell fusing at fertilisation, so each child gets a different mixture of genetic information from the two parents, and they also grow up in slightly different environments.

*Challenge*
1. e.g. fruit size or sweetness. The variety (genetic information) sets how big or sweet the tomatoes can be, but the amount of water, light, warmth and mineral salts the plant gets changes how big and sweet they actually grow. Accept mass, number of fruits or plant height with the same reasoning.
2. a) A bell shape: most students are in the middle groups (155 to 169 cm) and few are very short or very tall. This is a normal distribution. b) Height is continuous (any value) and is controlled by many genes plus the environment, so the values spread smoothly around a middle value; blood group has only four possible values, so its chart has separate bars with gaps and no curve. c) e.g. hand span, foot length, body mass, leaf length.

*My thinking:* Their environments are not identical: one may eat more, exercise more, get a scar, cut or style their hair differently, or get more sun. These are environmental variation, which does not depend on genetic information.

*Exit ticket*
1. In the nucleus (in the genes).
2. A zygote (fertilised egg cell).
3. Discontinuous; inherited.

## Lesson 3: Probability and statistics | 概率与统计

Student Book pp. 10 to 11 (9Ab Probability (Working Scientifically)) | Workbook pp. 7 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** Scientists use statistics to collect, analyse and present data, and probability (as a percentage, fraction or decimal) to say how likely something is, including inheriting a condition.

**Learning goals**
- I can explain what probability is and write a probability as a percentage, a fraction and a decimal.
- I can calculate an experimental probability from results and compare it with a theoretical probability.

**Key words**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| statistics | 统计 | collecting, analysing and presenting data |
| sample | 样本 | a small part of a group that is measured to estimate the whole group |
| mean | 平均数 | the sum of the values divided by the number of values |
| median | 中位数 | the middle value when the values are in order |
| mode | 众数 | the most common value in a set |
| probability | 概率 | the chance of something happening |
| percentage | 百分比 | a number out of 100, shown with the % sign |
| fraction | 分数 | a part of a whole, written as one number over another, e.g. 1/4 |
| decimal | 小数 | a number written with a decimal point, e.g. 0.25 |
| experimental probability | 实验概率 | a probability worked out from the results of an experiment |
| theoretical probability | 理论概率 | a probability worked out by reasoning about all the possible outcomes |

**Timeline**

| Phase | Min | Teacher does | Students do | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do Now | 4 | Weather forecast: what does 76% mean? Takes answers. | Answer the three questions in their books. | Slide 2 |
| Today | 1 | Reads the goals; points out that mean, median and mode are revision from maths. | Listen; find the key words. | Slide 3 |
| Hook | 3 | The dinosaur survey: why ask 113,867 people? | Pair talk, then share. | Slide 4 |
| Learn | 9 | Teaches what statistics is for; the probability scale and the three ways to write a probability; experimental versus theoretical; the sleeve-length normal distribution. Quick check. | Convert between %, fraction and decimal; answer the quick check. | Slides 5 to 9 |
| Do | 15 | Runs the two-dice investigation; circulates to check tallies and conversions. | Throw two dice 50 times, tally the scores, calculate experimental probabilities; Stretch and Challenge. | Slide 10, worksheet |
| Think and Reflect | 3 | Achondroplasia: is the third child certain to inherit it? | Pair discussion with the frames. | Slide 11 |
| Exit ticket | 3 | Collects exit tickets. | Complete the exit ticket alone. | Slide 12 |
| Summary | 2 | Recaps; previews DNA (Lesson 4). | Tick the goals they met. | Slide 13 |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** Match the averages and probability to their meanings; complete the probability scale; tally the dice scores and write each experimental probability as a fraction, decimal and percentage.
- **Stretch:** Interpret a 5% chance of rain; bead probabilities in three forms; compare experimental and theoretical probability for a score of 7.
- **Challenge:** Theoretical probabilities for achondroplasia and independent events; why 100 throws are more reliable than 10.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- 'A 76% chance of rain' means it rains for 76% of the time. It means that if that hour happened 100 times, it would rain on about 76 of them.
- After three sixes in a row, a six is 'due' or 'used up'. Each throw is independent: the theoretical probability stays 1/6.
- A 50% chance of inheriting a condition means that in a family of two children exactly one will have it. Each child has a 50% chance; two, one or neither may have it.

**Safety:** No practical hazards beyond normal classroom rules. Dice stay on the desks.

**Equipment**
- Two dice per pair (or a dice app on the teacher's device for a class demonstration)
- Worksheets (standard and Chinese key words versions)
- Calculators (optional)

**Oak National Academy**
- Variation (differences within species), Year 8: [Discontinuous variation: data handling and analysis](https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-secondary-ks3/units/variation-differences-within-species/lessons/discontinuous-variation-data-handling-and-analysis)
- Variation (differences within species), Year 8: [Continuous variation: data handling and analysis](https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-secondary-ks3/units/variation-differences-within-species/lessons/continuous-variation-data-handling-and-analysis)

**Slides and speaker notes**

| Slide | Type | Title | Speaker notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | title | Probability and statistics | A Working Scientifically lesson. The maths is revision; the science is using it to describe variation and to predict inherited conditions, which leads into genetic counselling in Lesson 5. |
| 2 | donow | Do Now: chance of rain | Expected: if that hour happened 100 times over, it would rain on about 76 occasions; 76/100 (or 19/25) and 0.76; not certain, because certain would be 100%. Four minutes, in books. |
| 3 | today | Today | Read the goals. Mean, median and mode are maths words the students know; today they are used on science data. |
| 4 | hook | Are you interested in dinosaurs? | The sample is large so that the results represent the whole population; the findings could decide whether to use dinosaurs in an advert. Twenty people might all be from one school or one age group. This is statistics: collecting, analysing and presenting data to draw conclusions and make predictions. |
| 5 | learn | What statistics is for | Work the example on the board (the list is six values for speed: mean = 537 divided by 6 = 89.5). A clothing company uses this to decide how many shirts of each size to make. |
| 6 | learn | Probability | Point along the scale. Ask for the probability of the Sun rising tomorrow (certain) and of finding a live dinosaur (impossible). Check conversions: 25% = 1/4 = 0.25. |

| 8 | chart | Probability from a normal distribution | Ask: is a random shopper more likely to need 85 cm or 95 cm sleeves? (About the same: 18 and 20.) Which value is the mean, median and mode here? (90 cm.) |

| 11 | think | Is the third child certain? | Expected: still 50%; each fertilisation is a separate event, like a new coin flip; certain would be 100%. Link to Lesson 5: a genetic counsellor explains these probabilities to families. |
| 12 | exit | Exit ticket | Answers: the chance of something happening; 0.05; 5/50 = 1/10 = 10%. |
| 13 | summary | What we learned | Homework: Workbook page 7 (9Ab Probability). |

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. mean: the sum of the values divided by the number of values; median: the middle value in a set of values in order; mode: the most common value in a set; probability: the chance of something happening.
2. 25%: 1/4, 0.25, unlikely (accept 'more unlikely'); 50%: 1/2; 75%: 3/4, 0.75; 100%: 1.0 (1).
3. a) Frequencies add up to 50; each fraction is frequency/50, the decimal is frequency divided by 50, the percentage is the decimal times 100 (e.g. 8/50 = 0.16 = 16%). b) Usually 7 (theoretically the most common score), but accept whatever the data show.

*Stretch*
1. B.
2. a) 5/50 = 1/10 = 0.1 = 10%. b) Not red: 30/50 = 0.6 = 60%.
3. Secure answer: the class values will be close to but not exactly 17%, e.g. 9/50 = 18%. Fifty throws is a small sample, so chance makes the experimental value differ from the theoretical one; with more throws (pooling the class results) the experimental probability gets closer to 17%.

*Challenge*
1. a) 0.5 and 1/2. b) No. Each child is a separate event (a different fertilisation), so the probability is still 50% for the third child; earlier children do not change it. c) They are worked out by reasoning about how the condition is inherited (the genetic information in the gametes), not by counting results from an experiment.
2. Numbers less than three: 1 and 2, so 2/6 = 1/3 = 0.33 (0.333...) = 33%. One hundred throws is a larger sample, so a run of lucky or unlucky throws has less effect and the experimental probability is closer to the theoretical value; with 10 throws one unusual throw changes the result by 10%.

*My thinking:* Disagree. Each throw is independent: the dice has no memory, so the theoretical probability of a six is still 1/6 on every throw. Three sixes in a row is unlikely (1/216) but it has already happened and does not change the next throw.

*Exit ticket*
1. The chance of something happening.
2. 0.05.
3. 5/50 = 1/10; 10%.

## Lesson 4: DNA, chromosomes and genes | DNA、染色体与基因

Student Book pp. 12 to 13 (9Ac DNA) | Workbook pp. 8 to 9 | 40 minutes

**Big idea:** Genetic information is stored in genes, which are sections of the long DNA molecules that make up the chromosomes in the nucleus; the structure of DNA was worked out in 1953 by scientists sharing evidence.

**Learning goals**
- I can outline how the structure of DNA was discovered and say what each scientist did.
- I can describe how DNA, chromosomes, genes and genetic information are linked, and give the number of chromosomes in body cells and gametes.

**Key words**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| DNA | DNA（脱氧核糖核酸） | the very long molecule that stores genetic information |
| double helix | 双螺旋 | the shape of a DNA molecule: two strands twisted into a spiral |
| chromosome | 染色体 | a structure in the nucleus made of one long DNA molecule |
| gene | 基因 | a section of DNA that carries the instructions for one characteristic |
| genetic information | 遗传信息 | the instructions for inherited characteristics |
| nucleus | 细胞核 | the part of a cell that contains the chromosomes |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** State the cause of inherited variation; put the lizard natural selection story in order; explain the peppered moth story and predict what happens when the soot is gone.
- **Stretch:** Rewrite Lamarck's explanation of long-legged wading birds as Darwin would; explain how salt tolerance was selected in the Salton Sea tilapia.
- **Challenge:** Explain how two separated populations can become different species; mammoth chromosomes, the evolution of thick hair, and why dinosaurs cannot be cloned.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- Organisms change themselves to suit the environment (Lamarck's idea: birds stretched their legs). Changes made during a lifetime are not inherited; natural selection only works on inherited variation that is already in the population.
- 'Only a theory' means evolution is a guess. In science a theory is an explanation that has been tested many times and is supported by a great deal of evidence.
- Natural selection always makes a species 'better'. It makes a population better adapted to its current environment; if the environment changes again, different variations are selected.

**Safety:** No practical hazards. For the paper-moth model, keep students seated except the chosen 'bird'; no running.

**Answer key**

*Core*
1. Genes (genetic information inherited from the parents through the gametes).
2. Order: 5, 3, 1, 4, 2 reading down the table. That is: when the river floods, the lizards with slightly longer legs run faster (1); the lizards with slightly longer legs are more likely to survive the flood (2); the surviving lizards produce offspring that also have longer legs (3); in the next generation, more of the lizards have longer legs (4); this process of natural selection repeats over and over again (5).
3. a) Secure answer: the soot made the trees black, so pale moths were easy for birds to see and were eaten, while black moths were camouflaged and more of them survived to reproduce and pass on the genes for black colour. b) The number of black moths has fallen (and pale moths have become more common again): on pale, lichen-covered trees the black moths are easier for birds to see, so more pale moths survive and reproduce, and each generation has more pale moths.

*Stretch*
1. Secure answer: the birds varied in leg length because of their genes (inherited variation). When fish were scarce, the birds with slightly longer legs could wade deeper, get more fish and were more likely to survive. They reproduced and passed on the genes for longer legs, so the next generation had more long-legged birds. This natural selection repeated over many generations until the birds had long legs. The birds did not stretch their legs; a change made during life is not inherited.
2. a) Being able to cope with (tolerate) very salty water. b) When the sea started getting salty, by chance some tilapia had genetic variations that helped them cope with salt. More of these fish survived than those that could not cope, so more of the salt-tolerant fish reproduced and the next generation had more salt-tolerant fish. As the sea got saltier this happened again and again, so today all the Salton Sea tilapia can cope with very salty water.

*Challenge*
1. Secure answer: the two populations live in different environments (different food, predators, climate), so different inherited variations are selected in each; over many generations each population evolves different characteristics. Eventually the two populations are so different that they can no longer breed together to produce offspring that can breed, which is the definition of a species, so they are now two species.
2. a) 29. b) The ancestor's population varied in hair length because of genes. As the climate got colder, individuals with slightly thicker hair kept warmer, so more of them survived and reproduced, passing on the genes for thick hair; over many generations the population evolved thick hair. c) Mammoth remains frozen in ice still contain DNA, which could be used for cloning; dinosaur DNA decomposed long ago (they died out 65 million years ago), so there is no genetic information to clone.

*My thinking:* In science a theory is an explanation that has been tested many times and is supported by a great deal of evidence (fossils, the peppered moth, bacteria becoming resistant to antibiotics). 'Only a theory' uses the everyday meaning (a guess), so it is not a good reason to doubt it.

*Exit ticket*
1. Organisms with inherited characteristics that suit their environment are more likely to survive, reproduce and pass on their genes, so those characteristics become more common.
2. A change over time in the characteristics of organisms (which can produce new species).
3. Soot from factories blackened the trees; birds could see and eat the pale moths more easily; more dark moths survived and reproduced, passing on their genes, so each generation had more dark moths.
