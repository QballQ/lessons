# Year 8 Admission Assessment v2: assessor booklet

**Version 2, October 2026.** This booklet goes with the Year 8 student paper, version 2. It replaces version 1 (`Y08_Assessor_Booklet`, now superseded and kept for reference). Version 2 adds challenge at the top so that the papers can tell competent students from fluent ones and place strong mathematicians accurately.

For students applying to join **Year 8** (age 12 to 13 on 1 September). A Year 8 entrant from a Chinese mainstream school has usually **completed Grade 6** (the end of primary school) and is the same age as a student starting Grade 7 (初一). The version 2 maths paper starts at the level expected on entry to Year 8 (the end of UK Year 7) and goes well beyond it, into Year 8 content, so expanding and factorising, equations with the unknown on both sides, inequalities, index laws, standard form, probability as a number and the mean from a frequency table **may not have been taught yet** (Section 4). A Year 8 entrant has usually done **no secondary science**: the science paper tests UK Year 7 content, so read the science score with the NT notes in Section 5. Background, the English bands and the decision guide are in `00_Admissions_Guide.md`.

## 2. Before you start

**Welcome script.** Say, in a calm, friendly voice:

> "Hello, I'm ... . Welcome to our school. Today you will do some activities in English: listening, reading, writing, maths and science. Then we will have a chat. The activities help us get to know you and find the right support for you. Some questions are easy and some are hard. That is fine. Do your best, and it is OK if you cannot answer everything. In maths and science I can read a question to you. In the English parts I cannot explain words, because we want to see your English. You can always ask me to say an instruction again. Do you have any questions?"

**Access rules** (the same in every year group; details in `00_Admissions_Guide.md`, Section 7)

| | English sections (listening, reading, writing, sentence reading) | Maths and science |
|---|---|---|
| Reading aloud | Read only what the script says. Do not explain words, translate or read the reading texts aloud. You may repeat instructions in English. | Read any question aloud in English, as often as asked, and point to the picture. |
| Chinese | Not used by staff. | If a Chinese-speaking colleague is present and the student cannot follow the English after it has been read aloud, the colleague may say the question **once** in Chinese. Mark the question **L** in the margin. The mark still counts. |
| Answers | **All answers must be in English** (numbers, ticks, drawings and working count). An answer in Chinese scores 0. | **All answers must be in English**, including the explanations in maths questions 14, 17b and 18. An answer or explanation in Chinese scores 0. Short phrases and numbers are enough. |
| Not yet taught | | If the student says they have not learnt a topic, write **NT** beside the question. It scores 0. List NT topics in the score summary. |

- **Extra time or a reader** for a known need is allowed; record it in the score summary.
- **Stopping.** The student may stop any section when they have done what they can. **Listening:** if you are assessing one student who clearly understood very little of Part A (a student new to English, for example), you may leave out Part B: say "Thank you, that is fine," and record "Part B not given"; the band then comes from Part A. With a group, read Part B to everyone and tell them it is hard. **Reading:** Part C is meant to stretch: if the student is clearly stuck after Part B, let them stop and say "Thank you, that is fine." **Writing:** if the student cannot write in English, ask them to write any words they know, or a plan; do not score Chinese writing, but keep it. Never push a distressed student: take a break or stop, and record it.
- **Keeping the student at ease.** Explain the order of the morning and the break. Sit beside, not opposite, for the interview. Praise effort, not answers ("Thank you, you are working hard"). Do not show surprise at mistakes. Keep the timer out of the student's line of sight, and give a calm two-minute warning near the end of each timed part. Many Year 8 students have just left primary school: tell them that listening Part B, reading Part C and the end of each maths section are meant to be hard, that the science has topics they may not have studied yet, and that this is expected.

## 3. English

### 3.1 Listening (about 10 minutes, 16 marks)

There are two parts. **Part A** is the everyday conversation from version 1, unchanged. **Part B** is a short talk at the level of a lesson for a fluent student of 12 to 13.

#### Part A: Mei and Amir talk about the charity fair (questions 1 to 8, about 4 minutes)

**Say:** "Turn to the Listening pages. There are two parts. In Part A, I am going to read a conversation between two students, Mei and Amir. I will read it two times. First, read questions 1 to 8." Give the student **one minute** to look at questions 1 to 8 (pages 2 and 3). Then say: "Now listen. You can answer while I read."

**Read it twice at a natural, steady pace.** You read both parts. Say the speaker's name before each turn, a little more quietly, and make the two voices slightly different. Pause for about 3 seconds at each [pause]. After the first reading, say "Now listen again" and read it again. After the second reading, give 30 seconds to finish. Do not repeat any part, and do not explain any word.

> **Script: Mei and Amir talk about the charity fair**
>
> **Amir:** Mei, have you decided what our class is going to sell at the charity fair?
>
> **Mei:** Yes! We wanted to sell cakes, but the Year 9 classes are already doing that. So we're going to sell plants instead: small ones, in pots. **[pause]**
>
> **Amir:** Plants? OK. When is the fair? Is it this Friday?
>
> **Mei:** No, it's next Saturday, from ten in the morning until two in the afternoon. **[pause]**
>
> **Amir:** And where is our stall? Next to the music stage again?
>
> **Mei:** No. Last year it was so loud there that nobody could hear us. This year we're by the main gate. **[pause]**
>
> **Amir:** What can I do to help?
>
> **Mei:** Can you make the price signs? You're much better at drawing than me. And Ms Chen says every class needs its own table. Can you bring one?
>
> **Amir:** Sure. I'll do the signs this weekend, and I'll ask my dad about a table. He's got a folding one at home. **[pause]**
>
> **Mei:** Thank you!
>
> **Amir:** Where will we get the plants?
>
> **Mei:** My aunt has a flower shop. She'll give us forty plants for 200 yuan. **[pause]**
>
> **Amir:** And how much will we sell them for?
>
> **Mei:** Ten yuan each. If we sell all of them, we'll get 400 yuan. After we pay my aunt, there will be 200 yuan for the charity. **[pause]**
>
> **Amir:** Great. Oh, but I can only come in the morning. I have a piano lesson at one o'clock. **[pause]**
>
> **Mei:** That's fine. And I'm really happy about the main gate.
>
> **Amir:** Me too. Everybody will walk past us when they come in!

**Answers.** One mark each. Spelling does not matter if the meaning is clear. Answers must be in English.

|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **B** plants | | A (cakes): heard "cakes" but missed "but the Year 9 classes are already doing that". |
| 2 | **C** 2:00 | | A (10:00) is the start; B (1:00) is Amir's piano lesson. |
| 3 | **B** (by the main gate) | | A: last year's place, next to the music stage. |

#### Part B: Dr Lin's talk about noise in cities (questions 9 to 16, about 6 minutes)

**Say:** "Now turn to Part B on page 4. I am going to read a short talk by a scientist, Dr Lin. She is talking to a class about noise in cities. I will read it two times. First, read questions 9 to 16. You have one minute." After one minute, say: "Now listen. You can answer while I read."

**Read it twice at a natural, steady classroom pace**, as a teacher would give a short talk: about 150 words a minute, so each reading takes about two and a half minutes. Pause for about 2 seconds at each [pause]. After the first reading, say "Now listen again" and read it again. After the second reading, give one minute to finish. Do not repeat any part, do not explain any word, and do not answer questions about the talk. Practise the talk aloud once before the day. Say these words clearly: **decibels** (DESS-ih-bels), **adrenaline** (a-DREN-a-lin), **chronic** (KRON-ik), **Munich** (MYOO-nik).

> **Script: Dr Lin's talk about noise in cities**
>
> Good morning, everyone. I'm Dr Lin, and I study how cities affect people's health. Today I want to talk about something most of us hardly notice: noise. **[pause]**
>
> We measure noise in decibels, a unit that tells us how loud a sound is. A quiet library is about forty decibels, a normal conversation is about sixty, and a busy road can reach eighty. People often say that ten more decibels make a sound ten times louder. Actually, that's not quite right. Ten more decibels means ten times more energy, but to our ears the sound only seems about twice as loud. **[pause]**
>
> So why does noise matter for health? When a sudden sound reaches your ears, your brain treats it as a warning of danger, even while you are concentrating on something else. As a result, your body releases stress hormones such as adrenaline, which make your heart beat faster and raise your blood pressure. This reaction helped our ancestors escape from predators, but in a city it can happen hundreds of times a day. Noise that continues for months or years is called chronic noise, and doctors have linked it to heart disease. **[pause]**
>
> Across Europe, one in five people live with traffic noise loud enough to harm their health. Noise affects learning, too: when Munich moved its airport in 1992, researchers tested children living near the old airport and the new one. Near the new airport, the children's reading and memory became worse, whereas near the old airport, which was now quiet, they improved. **[pause]**
>
> Interestingly, loudness is not the whole story. A fountain can be almost as loud as a busy street. However, most people find the sound of running water relaxing, while traffic makes them tense. **[pause]**
>
> So what can cities do? Electric cars are often presented as the answer. They are certainly quieter in slow traffic, but above about forty kilometres an hour, most noise comes from the tyres, not the engine. On the other hand, lower speed limits and quieter road surfaces help more than most people expect. **[pause]**
>
> In one new park beside a motorway, the noise level is only slightly lower than before, but the designers added several fountains, and visitors say they feel calmer there. **[pause]**
>
> So next time someone tells you that cities are simply noisy places, ask them: does it have to be that way?

**Answers.** One mark for each question; questions 12 and 13 are one mark each. Spelling does not matter if the meaning is clear. Answers must be in English.

| Q | Skill | Answer | Also accept | Do not accept, and what it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9 | Detail: a number | **60** | sixty; 60 decibels | 40 (a quiet library) or 80 (a busy road): heard the list of numbers but not which sound each one belongs to. |
| 10 | Detail: a correction | **about twice as loud** | | "about ten times as loud": heard "People often say ..." but missed the correction "Actually, that's not quite right". "A hundred times" is not said. |
| 11 | Process (sequence) | Your brain treats the sound as a warning of danger **2** · Your body releases stress hormones **3** · Your heart beats faster **4** | | All three correct for the mark. Heart before hormones: heard the words but not the chain of cause and effect ("As a result ... which make your heart beat faster"). |
| 12 | Note completion | **memory** | memories; any spelling that is clear (memery, memori) | "reading" (already in the notes); "learning" (said in the sentence before); "worse". |
| 13 | Note completion | **improved** | got better; became better; were better; went up | "got worse" (the new airport); "quiet" (the old airport became quiet, not the children's results); "the same". |
| 14 | Speaker's attitude (implied) | **She thinks they help, but other changes may help more.** | | "the best way to make cities quieter": the view she reports ("often presented as the answer") but does not share. "noisier": misheard "most noise comes from the tyres". |
| 15 | Inference joining two parts of the talk | The park has fountains, and (as Dr Lin said earlier) most people find the sound of running water relaxing, while traffic makes them tense. So the park feels calmer even though it is almost as loud. | They hear water, which is relaxing; the sound of the fountains is nicer or calmer than traffic; the fountains are relaxing; the water sound covers the traffic | "Because it is quieter" (she says the level is only slightly lower); "because there are fountains" with no idea of the sound being relaxing or pleasant; "because of the trees" (not said). |
| 16 | Main purpose | **to show that noise harms health, and that cities do not have to be so noisy** | | "how scientists measure sound" and "noise in different cities in Europe" are single details; "electric cars will solve the problem of noise" is the opposite of her view. |

**What the talk contains** (for the English team). 389 words in 25 sentences (average 15.6 words); 19% of the words are outside the 2000 most common English word forms (15% after simple ending rules, names left out; method as in `02_Listening_Evidence_Statement`, Section 6). Features: a process and a chain of cause and effect (questions 11 and 15), comparisons and contrasts (a fountain and a street; the old and new airports), numbers and a statistic, subject words explained in passing (decibels, hormones, chronic), a correction ("Actually, that's not quite right", question 10), an opinion that is implied, not stated (question 14), and discourse markers (as a result, however, whereas, on the other hand, interestingly). The facts are simplified but sound: decibels and loudness, the stress response, European traffic noise figures and the Munich airport study (children's reading and memory near the old and new airports, 1992 onwards).

**Band guide v2 (listening, / 16).** Use the part scores, in this order:

| Result | Band |
|---|---|
| Part A 0 to 2 | **A** |
| Part A 3 to 4 | **B** |
| Part A 5 to 6, or Part A 7 to 8 with Part B 0 to 2 | **C** |
| Part A 7 to 8 with Part B 3 to 5 | **D** |
| Part A 7 to 8 with Part B 6 to 8 | **E** |

Part A places a student in bands A to C; Part B separates C, D and E. If Part B is well above what Part A suggests (for example Part A 5, Part B 6), look for a slip in Part A, use judgement, and compare with "listening in conversation" on the interview record.

**What to look for**
- **Part A:** questions 1 to 4 each have a distractor that is mentioned in the conversation. Choosing the distractor every time shows a listener catching single words, not following the exchange: typical of band B. Question 6 needs the student to hear a correction ("after we pay my aunt"); questions 7 and 8 need inference across two turns.
- **Part B** is at the level of a lesson for a fluent student of 12 to 13. A fluent Year 8 listener usually scores 6 or more. Questions 10 and 14 test whether the student follows how the speaker positions an idea: a listener who catches single words chooses "ten times" and "the best way". Question 15 joins two parts of the talk, and question 16 the whole talk.
- **English barrier or something else?** A strong Part A with a weak Part B means the student copes with everyday English but not yet with academic English: plan support for subject language in lessons. This is different from a low Part A. If a student scores low in both but understood you well in conversation, note it and compare with the interview.

**What to look for**
- Part B (the sleep article) is age-appropriate for a fluent reader of 12. Part C is above age level: dense vocabulary (ingenious, precisely, possesses, resembles, humility), long sentences, figurative language ("melting into rock or coral") and an argument the reader must infer. A score of 5 or more in Part C shows reading well beyond everyday English.
- Strong readers answer questions 14, 19 and 20 in their own words and follow how the writer builds the argument: from a story, to a question, to evidence, to a point of view. Weaker readers copy a sentence near the key word whether or not it answers the question.

- Part B has subject vocabulary (melatonin, releases, developing, alert). Students from Chinese schools often know the science in Chinese; vocabulary, not the idea, is usually the barrier.
- **English barrier or reading difficulty?** If a student reads aloud fluently in the sentence reading screen but scores low here, the gap is vocabulary and comprehension, not decoding. If a student is slow and inaccurate in both, and also reads slowly in Chinese (ask the parents), note a possible reading difficulty.

### 3.3 Writing (10 minutes, 16 marks)

**Say:** "Turn to the Writing page. Choose one task and tick it. Plan in the box first. Then write. Try to write about 20 lines. You have 10 minutes." You may repeat the instruction. Do not explain the prompts.

**The tasks**
- **Task 1 (descriptive):** describe a busy place you know well, for example a train station at Spring Festival, a night market or a sports match.
- **Task 2 (persuasive):** a speech to the class persuading students to waste less food in the school canteen.

**Marking.** Score each criterion 0 to 4 (1 and 3 are between the descriptions). Total / 16. Then give the band by **best fit** with the descriptors in `00_Admissions_Guide.md`, Section 6, using the total only as a check.

| Criterion | 0 | 2 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Content and organisation** | Nothing in English, or isolated words; no link to the task. | A short response that follows the task; a list of things seen, or of reasons, with simple links ("and", "also"); little development; little paragraphing. | Well shaped for the age: the description builds a clear picture through the senses and has a focus (a moment, a mood); the speech has a clear position, two or three developed reasons with examples, a call to action and an awareness of the audience ("we", "you"); paragraphs. |
| **Vocabulary** | Single memorised words or copied words. | Everyday words used correctly; some repetition; gaps filled with simple words or Chinese. | A wide range, chosen for effect: precise nouns, verbs and adjectives in the description; persuasive language in the speech (rhetorical questions, "imagine", "surely", "every day we ..."); words for linking ideas. |
| **Grammar and sentences** | No sentences. | Simple sentences and some joined with "and", "but", "because"; frequent errors (verb tenses, plurals, articles, word order) but meaning is mostly clear. | Varied sentences, including complex ones and some for effect (a short sentence after a long one, a list of three); tenses controlled; errors are rare and do not affect meaning. |
| **Spelling and punctuation** | Spelling makes most words hard to read; no punctuation. | Common words spelt correctly; longer words phonetically plausible; full stops and capital letters used, not always correctly. | Accurate spelling, including most longer words; sentence punctuation secure; commas in lists and after openings, question and exclamation marks, apostrophes mostly correct. |

**Rough guide to the total** (best fit decides): 0 to 3 **A** · 4 to 7 **B** · 8 to 10 **C** · 11 to 13 **D** · 14 to 16 **E**.

**Band E writing at Year 8.** Comparable to a fluent writer of 12 or 13. In 10 minutes, usually 180 to 280 words in clear paragraphs. The description goes beyond a list: it uses the senses, chooses details and creates an atmosphere. The speech addresses its audience, argues with reasons and examples and ends with a clear call to action. Sentences are varied and controlled, vocabulary is precise and sometimes ambitious, and errors are few and minor. Judge quality, not just length.

**What to look for**
- Typical features of Chinese-speaking learners (missing past tense -ed or plural -s, "a" and "the", "he" and "she" mixed, "very" for every intensifier) are normal stages of learning English. Score them under grammar, but read past them to judge content.
- Memorised phrases from textbooks or exam practice ("As we all know", "In a word", "Last but not least") are common: they show learning, but judge the writing on what the student does with them.
- Writing much weaker than speaking is common in students from oral-heavy English courses; writing much stronger than speaking is common in students taught mainly through grammar and textbook dialogues.

### 3.4 Speaking and sentence reading (the interview, 15 minutes)

The interview is 1:1 and uses **`forms/Interview_Record_Y7_Y10`**. It opens with the **Graded Sentence Reading Screen** (`shared/Sentence_Reading_Record` and `shared/Sentence_Reading_Card`): for Year 8, **start at Level 7 (sentence 19)**, and follow the rules on the record for going back, stopping, the meaning checks and fluency. If you already know the student is new to English, start at Level 1. Then use the interview questions (school and learning, interests, friends and wellbeing, future plans and aspirations, values, an opinion question) and give the **speaking band** with the descriptors on the interview record. Copy the sentence reading total, reading age and the speaking band into the score summary.

The interview record's opinion questions (homework, phones) do not overlap with the writing tasks above.

## 4. Maths (25 minutes, 30 marks)

**Say:** "Turn to the Maths pages. There are three sections. Section A is calculation and algebra. Section B is word questions: read each one carefully and decide what to do. Section C is problems with pictures, tables and a map. You cannot use a calculator. Show your working in the boxes: you can get marks for your working. I can read any question to you. Just ask. You have 25 minutes."

Read any question aloud on request, as often as asked, and point to the picture. Follow the access rules (Section 2): mark **L** if a Chinese-speaking colleague gave a question once in Chinese, and **NT** if the student says they have not learnt the topic. **Section B is the evidence for the maths profile:** if you see a student stop at a word question, you may offer once, quietly, to read it aloud in English. Note which word questions were read aloud and which were given in Chinese (L).

**Marking.** Accept any clear, correct method. A correct answer with no working scores full marks, except in questions 14 and 18, where the working is the answer. Accept equivalent answers (for example 1/4 for 0.25) unless the question asks for a form (index form in question 1, standard form in question 9). Where a method mark is shown, give it for a correct method with one arithmetic slip. **ft** (follow through): where a later step uses an earlier wrong answer correctly, give the later mark. Where units are printed on the paper, a missing unit does not lose a mark. Explanations must be in English; short phrases with numbers are enough.

**Not covered in this paper:** simple interest, the circumference of a circle and the median and mode are not tested, because 30 marks cannot cover every topic.

**What to look for**
- **Fluency against word questions.** Questions 6 and 13 are a direct pair (the unknown on both sides, without and with words). A student who gets 6 but not 13, and then solves 13 when it is read aloud, has an English barrier, not a maths gap.
- **Explaining.** Students from Chinese schools often calculate quickly and accurately but write little working. In questions 14, 17b and 18 the explanation is part of the mark: short English with numbers is enough ("No. A is 432, B is 420."). If a student explains well aloud but writes nothing, note it: it shows maths understanding and a writing barrier.
- **Words that can block a maths question:** "off" (14), "without looking" and "altogether" (15), "consecutive" (11, explained on the paper), "average speed" and "scale" (16), "three quarters" and "show that" (18). Note any question that was solved after it was read aloud.
- A low score that is mostly Grade 7 and 8 algebra (4 to 9) or statistics and probability (15, 17) is very different from a low score on taught topics (2, 3, 10 to 12, 14, 16, 18). Say which in the summary.
