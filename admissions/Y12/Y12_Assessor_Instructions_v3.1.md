# Year 12 Admission Assessment v3.1: assessor instructions

**Version 3.1, October 2026.** Use with `Y12/Y12_Student_Paper_v3.1`. Year 12 joins the pack at version 3.1, so that every year group carries one version number: there is no earlier Year 12 paper. The core paper has the Years 7 to 10 structure (listening Parts A and B, reading Parts A and B, writing, maths in three sections, science) at the level of a student finishing IGCSE and starting A Level. Two further parts are given from their own booklets: the **cognitive ability screen** (`shared/Cognitive_Ability_Screen_Y11_Y12`, with its guide `shared/Cognitive_Ability_Screen_Guide`) and the **optional sciences** (`Y12/Y12_Optional_Sciences_v3.1`, with `Y12/Y12_Optional_Sciences_Mark_Scheme_v3.1`). Do not mix papers and instructions from different years or versions: the question numbers, marks and band guides differ.

**Reusable: print once and keep in the assessor's folder. Do not write on these pages.** Record each candidate's marks on their own **grading sheet** (`Y12_Grading_Sheet_v3.1`), reading on the **Reading front sheet** and the interview on the **Interview sheet**.

- **Listening** (16 marks): Part A is an everyday phone conversation (a school student and her cousin at university); **Part B** is a talk at the level of a lesson (turning seawater into drinking water).
- **Reading** (16 marks): Part A is a web page for a summer academy; Part B is an opinion article, "Lost, and Better for It" (questions 8 to 14), in the style of an IGCSE First Language English text.
- **Writing** (16 marks): a story, or an essay that must answer a counter-argument.
- **Maths** (30 marks): three sections that are scored separately: **Fluency** (10), **Word questions** (8) and **Applied problems** (12). A scientific calculator is allowed in Section C only.
- **Science** (10 marks): end-of-IGCSE level across biology, chemistry and physics, with one working-scientifically question.
- **Cognitive ability screen** and **optional sciences**: separate booklets, recorded on this year's grading sheet (Section 7).

**Who this is for.** Students applying for **Year 12** (age 16 on 1 September), the first year of the school's **A Level** course. A student from a Chinese mainstream school has usually **completed Grade 10 (高一)**: senior-high maths year 1 (sets, functions including exponential and logarithmic functions, trigonometric functions, vectors in two dimensions), physics (motion, forces, Newton's laws, energy), chemistry (the mole and amount of substance, redox, the periodic table) and biology (cells and molecules). They have usually **not** sat IGCSE. Some come from an international or bilingual school that teaches IGCSE: ask which syllabuses and which papers (Core or Extended) they have followed. A student joining mid-year is partway through Grade 11 (高二). Ask the student and the family which grade and which textbooks they have used, because the order of topics differs between textbooks and regions. Background on Chinese school sequences, the English bands and the decision guide is in `00_Admissions_Guide.md`.

**What it measures.** The student's level of English in listening, reading, writing and speaking (bands A to E) against the English that A Level study needs; their maths against the end of IGCSE Extended and the start of A Level Maths, reported as three sub-scores (fluency, word questions and applied problems); their core science against the end of IGCSE; the optional sciences against the subjects they want to take at A Level; and, through the cognitive ability screen, a profile for planning support (never for selection). Section 6 explains how to read the results together to judge **readiness for A Level study in English**.

## 2. Before you start

**Room.** Quiet, bright, a clock the student can see, the student paper face down, a pencil, rubber and ruler. Keep a scientific calculator for each student on your desk until they reach maths Section C. Keep the cognitive screen booklet and the optional sciences paper out of sight until their turn. Glasses or hearing aids if the student uses them.

**Say (welcome):** "Hello, I am [name]. Thank you for coming today. These activities help us get to know you, so that we can help you when you join us. Some questions are easy and some are hard. Nobody gets everything right, and that is fine. Do your best. If you do not know an answer, go on to the next question. Please write all your answers in English. In the English parts, I can only repeat the instructions. In maths and science, I can read any question to you. You can use a calculator only in the last section of the maths, and I will give it to you then. After this paper and a break, there are some shorter booklets. Do you have any questions before we start?"

**Pace of speech.** Year 12 students may still be learning English: speak steadily and clearly, with a short pause after each sentence, when you give instructions, read the listening scripts and read maths and science questions aloud. Use the same pace for every candidate, so that the results can be compared. The listening recording is paced the same way. Do not slow down for an older student who seems fluent: the pace is part of the measure.

#### Access rules (the same in every year group, with the calculator rule for Years 9 to 12)

| | English sections (listening, reading, writing, sentence reading) | Maths and science (core and optional); the cognitive screen |
|---|---|---|
| Reading aloud | Read only what the script says. Do not read the reading texts or questions aloud, explain words or translate. Instructions may be repeated in English. | Any question may be read aloud in English, as often as the student asks. You may point to the picture. In the cognitive screen, only the instructions and the worked examples may be read aloud or repeated, in English. |
| Chinese | Not used by staff. | If a Chinese-speaking colleague is present and the student clearly cannot follow the English after it has been read aloud, the colleague may say the question **once** in Chinese. Write **L** in the margin. The mark still counts. Record the number of L questions on the grading sheet: it is evidence about English. No Chinese in the cognitive screen. |
| Answers | **All answers in English.** An answer in Chinese scores 0. | **All answers in English**: numbers, symbols, words, ticks, drawings and working all count. An answer or explanation in Chinese scores 0 (this includes the "Explain" and "Show" answers in maths Section C and in science). |
| Calculator | Not used. | Maths Sections A and B: **no calculator**. Maths Section C: a **scientific calculator**. Core science: no calculator. Optional sciences: a scientific calculator. Cognitive screen: no calculator. |
| Not yet taught | | If the student says they have not learnt a topic, write **NT** beside the question. It scores 0. List NT topics on the grading sheet. |

Extra time, a reader or other arrangements for a known need are allowed: record them on the grading sheet. The cognitive screen is strictly timed; if extra time is given there, record it, because the scores are then not comparable.

#### Stopping rules

- The student may stop any section when they have done what they can. Say: "That is fine. Thank you. We will go on to the next part."
- **Listening:** if the student scores 2 or fewer out of 8 in Part A (questions 1 to 8), you may leave out Part B: check Part A quickly in the minute after it. Say: "Thank you. That is the end of the listening." Record "Part B not given" on the grading sheet; the band then comes from Part A alone (band A). In a group, play Part B to everyone as usual; a student with 2 or fewer in Part A may sit quietly.
- **Reading paper:** if the student cannot answer any of Part A after about 4 minutes, let them stop and record it on the Reading front sheet. Do not move them on to Part B.
- **Writing:** if the student has written nothing in English after 3 minutes, say once: "Write anything you can in English: a few words or sentences about yourself is fine." Then let them stop when they wish.
- **Maths and science:** encourage the student to look at every page, because later questions on different topics may be easier for them. Do not stop early unless the student is distressed. Give the calculator when the student turns to Section C (page 17); in a group, give each student theirs when they reach that page. If a student goes back to Section A or B after that, take the calculator back while they work on it.
- **Cognitive screen and optional sciences:** follow their own guides. A student whose English band from the morning is A or B still takes the cognitive screen: it is the part least dependent on English, and it is where such a student can show their reasoning.
- If a student becomes upset at any point, stop, take a break, and continue only if they are happy to. Record it on the grading sheet.

**Keeping the student at ease.** Smile, use the student's name, and keep your voice calm. Do not say "right" or "wrong" during the tasks; say "thank you" instead. Give a two-minute warning before the end of the reading paper, maths and science. A student who finishes early may check their work quietly.

## 3. English

### 3.1 Listening (16 marks: Part A 8, Part B 8)

There are two parts, and each is played or read twice. **Part A** is everyday listening: a phone conversation between a school student and her cousin, a university student. **Part B** is a short lesson-style talk with subject vocabulary, statistics, a correction and an implied opinion: the listening that A Level lessons need, at the level of a fluent speaker of 16 to 17.

**Recording.** A recording of this test is in `audio/v3.1/Y12/` (see its `READ_ME_FIRST.txt` for the tracks and timings). Play it instead of reading the script: every candidate then hears the same voices, pace and pauses. The script below is its transcript, and the live reading option if no recording can be played. Read live at the recording's pace: one reading of Part A takes about 2 minutes 24 seconds and of Part B about 3 minutes 27 seconds, with the pauses; use this to check your own pace. Do not pause the recording during a reading of the script. Play Part A and Part B as separate tracks if you may stop after Part A; otherwise the full track plays both.

**Part A. Say:** "Turn to the Listening page. There are two parts. In Part A, you will hear a phone conversation between Li Na, a school student, and her cousin Chen Yu, who is at university. I will play it two times. First, you have one minute to read questions 1 to 8." Wait one minute. **Say:** "Now listen." Play or read the Part A script. Wait 10 seconds. **Say:** "Now listen again." Play or read it again. Then give the student one more minute to finish.

**Part B. Say:** "Now turn to Part B, on page 4. You will hear a talk by an engineer, Dr Wang Hao, about turning seawater into drinking water. I will play it two times. First, you have one minute to read questions 9 to 16." Wait one minute. **Say:** "Now listen." Play or read the Part B script. Wait 10 seconds. **Say:** "Now listen again." Play or read it again. Then give the student one more minute to finish.

**Read both scripts twice at the steady pace in Section 2.** In Part A, say each speaker's name before their line, a little more quietly, both times. In Part B, read as a teacher gives a short lesson, without slowing down for the numbers or the subject words. At each [pause], stop for about 3 seconds. Do not repeat lines or explain anything.

**Timing.** The recording takes about 17 minutes: Part A about 7 minutes 42 seconds and Part B about 9 minutes 41 seconds, with one minute to read each set of questions, the 10-second gaps between the two readings and one minute to finish each part. **Allow about 17 minutes** for the listening.

#### Script, Part B: a talk about turning seawater into drinking water

Good afternoon. My name is Dr Wang Hao, and I'm an engineer. I design plants that turn seawater into drinking water. [pause]

Only about two and a half per cent of the water on Earth is fresh, and most of that is frozen in ice caps and glaciers. Everything else is salty. A litre of seawater contains about thirty-five grams of salt: roughly six teaspoons. Drinking water should contain almost none. So the challenge is to remove nearly all the salt, cheaply, from an enormous amount of water. [pause]

There are two main ways. The oldest is distillation: you boil the seawater, the steam leaves the salt behind, and you cool the steam back into pure water. It works, but boiling water needs a great deal of energy. Most modern plants use a different method, called reverse osmosis. Osmosis is the natural movement of water through a very thin skin, or membrane, from the weaker solution to the stronger one. Reverse osmosis forces it the other way: seawater is pushed against the membrane at very high pressure, pure water passes through, and the salt stays behind. [pause]

Let me walk you through the plant my team studied last year. First, seawater is pumped in through a pipe that lies on the sea floor, some way out from the beach. Second, the water passes through sand filters, which remove sand, seaweed and tiny living things that would block the membranes. Third comes the reverse osmosis itself. Finally, before the water goes to the city, a small amount of minerals is added back, because completely pure water tastes flat, and a little chlorine protects it in the pipes. [pause]

That plant produces one hundred thousand cubic metres of drinking water a day. Actually, let me correct that: it was a hundred thousand when it opened. Since the second stage was added, it produces one hundred and fifty thousand, which is enough for about half a million people. [pause]

Now the costs. For every two litres of seawater that enter, roughly one litre of drinking water comes out. The other litre, now twice as salty, is called brine, and it is returned to the sea. Brine is denser than seawater, so it sinks and spreads along the sea floor. Most sea creatures live near the shore, in shallow water, so the brine has to be released slowly and far out. The second cost is electricity. The pumps use a great deal of it, and at that plant the water costs about twice as much as river water. [pause]

Some people now say that a coastal city with a desalination plant never needs to worry about water again. I notice that the same city loses nearly a fifth of its water through leaking pipes before it reaches a single tap. I would rather fix the pipes first. [pause]

The sea is not going to run out. But turning it into a drink has a price, paid in energy and in the health of the coast. Whether a city should pay it is a question for all of us, not only for engineers. Thank you. [pause]
[[/passage]]

**What is in the Part B script** (for the English team): about 510 words in 36 sentences (about 14 words a sentence on average); about 20% of the word forms are outside the 2000 most common English words (`wordfreq` list, names left out). It explains a process (reverse osmosis and the four stages of the plant); compares distillation with reverse osmosis, seawater with brine, and desalinated water with river water; gives statistics; explains subject vocabulary in context (osmosis, membrane, reverse osmosis, pre-treatment, brine, desalination); corrects a number ("Actually, let me correct that"); implies opinions without stating them (the people who say a city never needs to worry; the leaking pipes; who should decide); and uses discourse markers (first, second, third, finally, in other words, now, so, but).

If the two parts disagree in an unexpected way (for example Part A 5 or 6 with Part B 5 or more), use the interview to decide: the student may have lost easy marks in Part A through nerves or carelessness.

#### What to look for

- **Part A** checks everyday listening: questions 1 to 3 the main points, 4 to 6 exact details with distractors (five other students, twice as long, twenty yuan), and 7 and 8 implied meaning and the speaker's purpose.
- **Part B** is the step up. Questions 9 to 13 need exact detail from fast, connected speech, with distractors (2.5%, chlorine, the order of the stages, 100 000); questions 14 to 16 need the student to join ideas, hear an attitude that is never stated and see the purpose of the whole talk. A student who gets 9 to 13 but not 14 to 16 follows the content of a lesson but not yet its argument, which is the skill A Level lessons and essays depend on.
- **Part A secure, Part B 0 to 2**: everyday English is fine, but lessons taught only by talk will be hard to follow at first. Plan visual support, key vocabulary in writing and frequent checks for understanding.
- Compare with listening in the interview. If the two differ by more than one band, use the interview as a check.

#### What to look for

- **Part B** is the hardest text on this paper and the closest to what A Level reading demands: an argument built from an anecdote, a concession ("I can hear the objection"), evidence and a modest proposal. A Part B score of 7 or 8 with answers in the student's own words shows a reader who can follow an argument in English and say what a writer is doing.
- **Strong readers** reword rather than copy (8 and 14), give evidence for an inference (11), and can say what a comparison does rather than only what it means (12). Copying whole lines that are in the right place but do not answer the question is the commonest sign of a band C or D reader.
- **An English barrier** looks like: Part A secure, Part B patchy or copied lines. A student who reads aloud well (sentence reading) but scores low on Part B may be decoding without understanding: see the meaning checks on the Reading front sheet.
- **Speed.** Note where the student was when time ran out, on the Reading front sheet. A Level and IGCSE papers are long; a student who is accurate but very slow will need reading practice and, possibly, an access arrangement assessment if there is a specific need.

### 3.3 Writing (10 minutes, 16 marks)

**Say:** "Turn to the Writing page. Choose one task, A or B, and tick the box. You can plan in the box, then write on the lines. You have 10 minutes. Write as much as you can." Do not explain the tasks or words.

**The tasks.** Task A (narrative): a story that ends with the words "and for the first time, I was glad I had stayed." Task B (argumentative): an essay for a student magazine on whether exam results are the only fair way to choose students for university, with an opinion, reasons and examples, and **at least one argument against the writer's view, answered**. The plan is not marked. Task B is the demand of an A Level entrant: an essay that recognises and answers the other side.

**Score each criterion 0 to 4** (1 and 3 fall between the descriptions).

| Criterion | 0 | 2 | 4 (what a fluent 16 year old would do) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Content and organisation** | Nothing relevant in English, or a few words copied from the task. | Relevant to the task; ideas in a sensible order but little developed; may be one block of text; the story is a sequence of events with little shape, or the essay lists reasons without explaining them and ignores the other side. | Well developed and clearly organised in paragraphs. Story: a narrator with a reason to leave, a turning point, and an ending that earns the final sentence; some control of pace and detail. Essay: a clear position, reasons developed with examples, **a counter-argument stated fairly and answered**, a conclusion; written for magazine readers. |
| **Vocabulary** | Isolated words, or only words from the prompt. | Everyday words used correctly but repeated ("very important", "good", "many people"); a few words chosen for effect; simple linking (and, but, because). | Wide and precise vocabulary chosen for effect (in the story: sensory and emotional detail; in the essay: the language of argument: claim, evidence, however, admittedly, nevertheless, on balance); a range of linking words; figurative language that works. |
| **Grammar and sentences** | No sentences. | Mostly simple sentences, some joined with and, but, because; frequent errors in verb forms, plurals and articles, but the meaning is clear. | A variety of sentence types used for effect, including complex sentences and some short sentences for impact; tenses controlled across the piece; errors are rare and do not affect meaning. |
| **Spelling and punctuation** | Mostly impossible to read. | Common words spelt correctly; capital letters and full stops mostly correct; errors in less common words. | Almost all words spelt correctly, including ambitious ones; a range of punctuation (commas after openers and in lists, speech marks in the story, apostrophes, colons and semicolons) used correctly. |

**Band by best fit**, using the descriptors in `00_Admissions_Guide.md`, Section 6, and the notes below. The total is a check, not a rule: as a rough guide, totals of 0 to 3 usually go with A, 4 to 7 with B, 8 to 10 with C, 11 to 13 with D and 14 to 16 with E.

| Band | Writing at Year 12 entry |
|---|---|
| **A** | Name, isolated words or copied phrases; may write in Chinese. |
| **B** | A few short sentences on the topic, with frequent errors; meaning mostly clear. |
| **C** | A short connected piece (often 70 to 130 words) with some joined sentences; errors frequent but rarely block meaning; little paragraphing; the essay gives an opinion with one or two reasons and no counter-argument. |
| **D** | An organised piece (often 130 to 220 words) with paragraphs and a range of sentences; generally accurate with occasional errors; some varied vocabulary; the essay mentions the other side, even if it does not answer it well. |
| **E** | See below. |

**Band E writing at this age.** A fluent 16 year old usually writes 200 to 300 words in 10 minutes, in paragraphs, with controlled, varied sentences, precise vocabulary chosen for its effect on the reader, and accurate spelling and punctuation. The story creates a narrator and a turning point rather than listing events; the essay argues, uses examples, states the opposing view fairly and shows why it does not change the conclusion. This is roughly the level an A Level essay-based subject starts from.

#### What to look for

- Typical features of English from speakers of Chinese (missing articles, plural -s, past tense -ed, third person -s) are normal at bands C and D. Do not let them alone pull a well-organised, well-developed piece below D.
- A memorised opening ("Nowadays, with the development of society") or memorised essay phrases followed by very simple writing shows exam training more than fluency: band by what the student can produce independently.
- **The counter-argument** (Task B) is the A Level skill. A student who states the other side fairly and answers it, even in imperfect English, is showing the thinking that essay subjects need; a student with fluent English who ignores the instruction is showing something else. Note it in the assessor's comment either way.
- For A Level, the gap between the writing band and the other bands matters most: essay subjects (history, economics, English, business, psychology) are assessed almost entirely through extended writing. If writing is two bands below reading, name it in the assessor's comment on the grading sheet.

**Say:** "Turn to the Maths page. You have 25 minutes. There are three sections. In Sections A and B, you may not use a calculator. A student from an IGCSE school should have met all of it. Use NT and judge whether the student would reach the expected band once taught: a strong 高一 student usually learns these quickly.

"Grasshopper" (the primary consumer) or "snake" (the tertiary consumer) scores 0; note which, because both show the chain was understood but not the term. | Food chains are Grade 8 biology (生态系统); the term "secondary consumer" in English is the thing to check. |
| 2 | Series circuits: resistance and current | (a) 12 Ω (b) 0.5 A | (a) B1. 2.67 Ω (resistors treated as parallel) scores 0. (b) B1 for 0.5 (6 ÷ 12), following through from their total (6 ÷ 2.67 = 2.25 A scores B1 only if (a) was 2.67). 1.5 A (6 ÷ 4) or 0.75 A (6 ÷ 8) scores 0. | Secure (Grade 9 physics, 串联电路 and 欧姆定律). |
| 3 | The mole: moles from mass | 0.25 mol | B1. (*M*<sub>r</sub> = 24 + 16 = 40; 10.0 ÷ 40.) An answer of 0.42 (10 ÷ 24, the oxygen forgotten) or 400 (10 × 40) scores 0. | Secure (高一 chemistry, 物质的量). |
| 4 | Working scientifically: pattern, variables, evaluating a method | (a) Height increases with fertiliser up to 2 g per litre, then decreases (b) One control variable (c) One sensible improvement | (a) B1: both parts of the pattern needed (the rise and the fall); "more fertiliser, taller plant" alone scores 0 because it ignores 3 and 4 g per litre. (b) B1: the same type (species) of plant, the same size of plant at the start, the same amount (volume) of water, the same soil, the same pot, the same light, the same temperature, the same time (4 weeks). Do not accept "the fertiliser" or "the height". (c) B1: grow several plants at each concentration and find the mean height; repeat the experiment; measure the mass (dry mass) as well as height; use more concentrations between 1 and 3. Do not accept "do it more carefully". | Fertiliser and plant growth are Grade 7 biology. Evaluating a method is less familiar: score what the student writes. |
| 5 | The wave equation | 20 m/s | B1. (50 × 0.4.) 125 (50 ÷ 0.4) or 0.008 scores 0. | *v* = *f*λ is 高二 physics (机械波): may not have been taught, but the equation is given on the paper, so a student can read the wavelength (0.4 m) from the diagram and multiply. |
| 6 | Ionic compounds: conduction when molten | Ions can move when molten (free to move, carry the charge); in the solid, the ions are held in fixed positions (in a lattice) and cannot move | B1 for the idea that the **ions** move when molten and not when solid. "Electrons move" scores 0 (this is the common confusion with metals). "Because it melts" scores 0. | Secure (高一 chemistry, 电解质 and 离子键; Grade 9 introduces ions). |
| 7 | Gas exchange: adaptations of the alveolus | One feature with its reason: a wall one cell thick (a short distance for diffusion); a capillary right next to it or a good blood supply (keeps the concentration gradient steep, carries gases away quickly); a large surface area from many alveoli (more diffusion at once); a moist surface (gases dissolve) | B1 for a feature **and** why it helps. A feature alone ("thin wall") scores 0 at this level; "it has air in it" scores 0. | Secure (Grade 7 biology, 呼吸系统; 高一 biology covers diffusion across membranes). The English of "feature" and "gas exchange" is the thing to check. |

**Band guide** (provisional): 0 to 3: **1**; 4 to 5: **2**; 6 to 8: **3**; 9 to 10: **4**. Look at the NT questions before deciding. For the sciences the student will take at A Level, the optional papers (15 marks each) are the better evidence: see Section 6.

**Not tested on the core paper** (not enough time): genetics, homeostasis, enzymes, rates of reaction, electrolysis, organic chemistry, magnetism, radioactivity, momentum. The optional sciences cover them.

#### What to look for

- **Working scientifically** (question 4) is the best guide to A Level practical work: a student who sees the rise and the fall in the data, names a control variable and suggests a real improvement is ready for practical work in English.
- **An English barrier** looks like: blank or wrong answers that become right when you read the question aloud. Science vocabulary in English (secondary consumer, resistance, moles, variable, molten, feature) is often the gap: note the words the student did not know in the assessor's comment on the grading sheet.
- **Strong responses** use units (Ω, A, mol, m/s), give both halves of an explanation (question 7) and give precise answers in question 4 (for example "grow five plants at each concentration and calculate the mean").

## 6. Readiness for A Level

**This section is guidance for the school to confirm**, against its own admissions policy, the A Level subjects and syllabuses it offers, the entry requirements it sets for each subject, the university destinations its students aim for, and the support it can provide. It is not a set of fixed rules. Record the results on the grading sheet (Section 7) first, then read the results together.

**English is the gateway.** Every A Level subject is taught and examined in English, with long texts, extended written answers and essay questions. The demand is higher than at IGCSE, and there is less time: a two-year A Level course leaves little room for English to catch up. Look at the overall English band, and most closely at **reading and writing**, because the exams depend on them. Academic English usually develops more slowly than conversation: as a planning assumption, a student rarely moves up more than about one band a year in academic reading and writing, even with good support. A student at band C at the start of Year 12 is likely still to be at C or D when the exams come.

| English band | What it usually means for A Level study in English | English course that may fit best |
|---|---|---|
| **A** New to English | Cannot yet follow subject lessons in English. Direct entry to A Level is not usually advisable (`00_Admissions_Guide.md`, Section 9). | A preparatory or foundation route, or a later start, if the school has one. |
| **B** Early acquisition | Can follow simple, supported lessons, but A Level texts and exam questions will be out of reach for most of the first year. | Direct entry to A Level is not usually advisable; a one-year pre-A Level or IGCSE year with intensive English, if the school has one. |
| **C** Developing competence | Can follow most lessons with support; subject vocabulary and extended writing will slow progress. Maths and the sciences are more accessible than essay-based subjects. | **English as a Second Language support** alongside A Levels in maths and the sciences only; essay-based subjects not advised in the first year, or a pre-A Level year. |
| **D** Competent | Usually ready for A Level courses taught in English, with support for academic writing; essay-based subjects possible with support. | English as a Second Language support at the higher level in the first year; First Language level if reading and writing are a strong D or E. |
| **E** Fluent | Ready for A Level study in English, including essay-based subjects. | **First Language level** (no ESL course needed); English Literature or Language at A Level if the student wants it. |

**What English band does a student realistically need?** As a working guide for the school to test and review: **band D or above in reading and writing** for essay-based subjects (for example economics, business, history, psychology, English), and **band C or above in reading and writing, with English as a Second Language support**, for maths and the sciences. Listening and speaking at C or above in either case. Review these thresholds once the first groups of students have taken their A Levels.

**What the version 3.1 English results add.** Use the hardest parts as the evidence for A Level:

- **Listening Part B** is the best guide to following lessons taught by talk. Part B 6 or more: the student can follow a lesson-style explanation with subject vocabulary and hear its argument. Part B 0 to 2 with Part A secure: everyday listening is fine, but lessons will need visual support and key vocabulary in writing at first.
- **Reading Part B** ("Lost, and Better for It") is a First Language-style opinion article and the hardest reading on this paper. Part B 7 or 8, answered in the student's own words, with writing at E or a strong D: reads like a fluent speaker and is ready for essay-based subjects. Part B 6 or more otherwise: a competent reader who will need support with the longest A Level texts.
- **Writing Task B** (the essay with a counter-argument) is the closest task on this paper to an A Level essay. A clear position, a fairly stated counter-argument and an answer to it, in organised paragraphs, is the mark of readiness for essay subjects even when the English has band D errors.

**Maths: A Level Maths, and what support.** Read the total band with the three sub-scores (Section 4), allowing for NT. A Level Maths assumes the whole of IGCSE Extended (or its equivalent) and fluent algebra.

| Maths result | What it suggests (for the school to confirm) |
|---|---|
| Band 4 (23 or more) | **A Level Maths: suitable**, likely top set. Discuss **Further Maths** if the school offers it, especially if questions 4, 5 and 16 are right. |
| Band 3 (17 to 22) | **A Level Maths: suitable.** NT marks on differentiation, inverse functions, sequences and cumulative frequency are expected for a 高一 student and are usually caught up in the first term. |
| Fluency 7 or more, word questions 3 or less (any band) | **A Level Maths with support for the English of maths**: maths vocabulary, reading problems, writing short explanations in English. The gap is English, not maths: do not advise against A Level Maths for this reason alone. |
| Fluency and word questions secure, applied 5 or less | **A Level Maths with support** in problem solving, reading graphs and explaining ("show that", "explain"). Review after one term. |
| Band 2 (11 to 16), mostly NT and L | **A Level Maths is still possible**: judge the band once taught, and review after six weeks. |
| Band 2 with errors on taught topics (questions 1, 2, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11, 15) | **A Level Maths not advised** without a catch-up plan; a pre-A Level year, or A Level subjects that do not depend on maths, may be wiser. Review if the gaps close. |
| Band 1 not explained by NT or English | Further assessment before a decision (`00_Admissions_Guide.md`, Section 9). A band 1 that NT or L does explain is treated as band 2 (the guide, Section 9). |

**The optional sciences and A Level sciences.** Each optional paper (15 marks, band 1 to 4 from `Y12_Optional_Sciences_Mark_Scheme_v3.1`) is the main evidence for that A Level science. As a working guide: band 3 or 4 in a science suggests **suitable** for that A Level; band 2 suggests **with support** (a content catch-up plan for the NT topics named in the mark scheme, and a review after one term); band 1 not explained by NT suggests **not advised** for that science. Read each science with the maths result (physics and chemistry at A Level depend on algebra) and with the English band (every science has extended-answer questions). A science the student did not sit cannot be judged: tick "not taken" and do not infer it from the others.

**The cognitive ability screen and support planning.** The screen is **not used to select**. Use its profile (`shared/Cognitive_Ability_Screen_Guide`) to plan support: non-verbal and quantitative both 9 or more with verbal low usually means strong reasoning and an English gap (plan language support, and expect the paper results to rise as English does); processing speed well below the other parts suggests checking reading fluency and considering extra time; all three reasoning parts 4 or less suggests discussing further assessment before a decision (`00_Admissions_Guide.md`, Section 9); a strong profile with weak paper results suggests looking for gaps in prior learning or motivation. Write the profile note on the grading sheet.

**When a one-year pre-A Level or IGCSE route is wiser.** Consider it, if the school offers one, when: English is band B, or band C with essay-based subjects wanted; the student has not sat IGCSE and the maths is band 2 on taught topics; the optional sciences for the subjects wanted are band 1 or 2 with many NT topics; or the interview shows that the student does not yet understand what A Level study in English involves. A year of IGCSE in English usually raises the English band, fills the content gaps and produces the IGCSE grades that universities and the school's own entry requirements may ask for. Tell the family early what the school's requirements are.

**Reading the results together** (examples of common profiles)

| Profile | What it suggests | A possible plan (for the school to confirm) |
|---|---|---|
| English D or E; maths 3 or 4; optional sciences 3 or 4 in the subjects wanted | Ready for a full A Level programme. | Full subject load; English course by the table above; A Level Maths. |

| English C; maths 2 with NT on differentiation, sequences and cumulative frequency; sciences 2 with NT topics | Likely to manage with support, or better placed in a pre-A Level year. | If entry: ESL support, catch-up plans for the NT topics, maths and sciences reviewed after six weeks. Otherwise a pre-A Level or IGCSE year. |
| English B, any maths and sciences | The English gap is too wide for A Level content in two years. | A pre-A Level or IGCSE year with intensive English, if the school has one; otherwise discuss a later start (`00_Admissions_Guide.md`, Section 9). |

A serious mismatch, or any strand rated 1, goes to the head of section before any offer. |

Use the Year 11 and 12 interview questions (subject choices and why, university plans, independent study, managing a heavy workload, what the student knows about A Level) to judge whether the student's plans are realistic and their own. Record the readiness judgement on the grading sheet (the readiness for A Level boxes and the assessor's comment) and on the final mark sheet (`forms/Candidate_Summary_and_Decision`).

## 7. Recording the results

1. **Mark the student paper.** Every question has a mark box.
2. **Copy the totals onto the grading sheet** (`Y12_Grading_Sheet_v3.1`) and give the bands from the band guides above (the grading sheet repeats them at a glance). Record there too whether Part B of the listening was given, the maths sub-scores and profile (Section 4), the NT topics and L questions, the writing task chosen (A or B) and whether the essay answered a counter-argument, any access arrangements and your comment.
3. **Mark the optional sciences** with `Y12_Optional_Sciences_Mark_Scheme_v3.1` and record each science taken on the grading sheet (score / 15, band, NT, L); tick "not taken" for each science the student did not sit.
4. **Score the cognitive ability screen** with `shared/Cognitive_Ability_Screen_Guide` and record the four raw scores (Non-verbal / 12, Quantitative / 12, Verbal / 12, Processing speed / 60) and a one-line profile note on the grading sheet. It is not banded and it is not used to select.
5. **Record the reading results and band on the Reading front sheet** (`forms/Reading_Front_Sheet`): the reading paper marks (Part A, Part B), the band and the question where the student stopped at 15 minutes.
6. **Give the interviewer the Reading front sheet and the Interview sheet** before the interview.
7. **After the interview, put together** the grading sheet, Reading front sheet, Interview sheet and parent conversation record, and complete the final mark sheet (`forms/Candidate_Summary_and_Decision`) within two working days (or within two working days of the second visit, if there is one). Write "v3.1" beside the scores on it: the listening is out of 16, the reading paper is out of 16, the maths has three sub-scores, each optional science is out of 15 and the cognitive screen is four raw scores. The overall English band is the band most skills sit in: name any skill that is two or more bands lower.
8. **File the marked papers with them** (or shred them under the school's policy).

**Year 12 only: readiness for A Level.** Read the results together with Section 6, then tick on the grading sheet the English course (English as a Second Language support, First Language level or to decide), A Level Maths (suitable, with support, not advised or to decide), the sciences (suitable for the A Level sciences taken, with support, or not advised), the readiness (Ready, Ready with support, Pre-A Level year advised or Not yet) and the support (English of maths, academic writing, content gaps, extra time to check, or none), and give your reasons in the assessor's comment.
