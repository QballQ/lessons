# Year 10 Admission Assessment v3.1: assessor booklet

**Version 3.1, October 2026.** Use with `Y10/Y10_Student_Paper_v3.1`. Version 3.1 is version 2 with the third reading text (Part C) removed: the listening, writing, maths, science and interview are unchanged from version 2. The reading paper is now Parts A and B (16 marks) in the same 15 minutes. Do not mix papers and booklets from different versions: the question numbers, marks and band guides differ.

- **Listening** (16 marks): Part A is the version 1 talk, unchanged; **Part B** is the academic talk at the level of a lesson.
- **Reading** (16 marks): Part A is the version 1 menu; Part B is the version 1 article "Ten Minutes of Nothing" (questions 8 to 14).
- **Maths** (30 marks): three sections that are scored separately: **Fluency** (10), **Word questions** (8) and **Applied problems** (12). A scientific calculator is allowed in Section C only.
- **Writing, science, the interview and the shared files are unchanged.**

[[namebox:candidate]]

**Who this is for.** Students applying for **Year 10** (age 14 on 1 September), the first year of a **two-year IGCSE course**. A student from a Chinese mainstream school has usually **completed Grade 8 (初二)**; a student joining mid-year is partway through Grade 9 (初三). Ask the student and the family which grade and which textbooks they have used, because the order of topics differs between textbooks and regions. Background on Chinese school sequences, the English bands and the decision guide is in `00_Admissions_Guide.md`.

**What it measures.** The student's level of English in listening, reading, writing and speaking (bands A to E); their maths against all of Key Stage 3 and the start of the IGCSE Extended course, reported as three sub-scores (fluency, word questions and applied problems); their science against the end of Key Stage 3 (the UK Year 9 curriculum); and, through the interview, who they are, their future plans and their suitability for the programme. Section 6 explains how to read the results together to judge **readiness for IGCSE study in English**.

## 2. Before you start

**Room.** Quiet, bright, a clock the student can see, the student paper face down, a pencil, rubber and ruler. Keep a scientific calculator for each student on your desk until they reach maths Section C. Glasses or hearing aids if the student uses them.

**Say (welcome):** "Hello, I am [name]. Thank you for coming today. These activities help us get to know you, so that we can help you when you join us. Some questions are easy and some are hard. Nobody gets everything right, and that is fine. Do your best. If you do not know an answer, go on to the next question. Please write all your answers in English. In the English parts, I can only repeat the instructions. In maths and science, I can read any question to you. You can use a calculator only in the last section of the maths, and I will give it to you then. Do you have any questions before we start?"

#### Access rules (the same in every year group, with the version 2 calculator rule)

| | English sections (listening, reading, writing, sentence reading) | Maths and science |
|---|---|---|
| Reading aloud | Read only what the script says. Do not read the reading texts or questions aloud, explain words or translate. Instructions may be repeated in English. | Any question may be read aloud in English, as often as the student asks. You may point to the picture. |
| Chinese | Not used by staff. | If a Chinese-speaking colleague is present and the student clearly cannot follow the English after it has been read aloud, the colleague may say the question **once** in Chinese. Write **L** in the margin. The mark still counts. Record the number of L questions: it is evidence about English. |
| Answers | **All answers in English.** An answer in Chinese scores 0. | **All answers in English**: numbers, symbols, words, ticks, drawings and working all count. An answer or explanation in Chinese scores 0 (this includes the "Explain" and "Show" answers in maths Section C). |
| Calculator | Not used. | Maths Sections A and B: **no calculator**. Maths Section C: a **scientific calculator**. Science: no calculator. |
| Not yet taught | | If the student says they have not learnt a topic, write **NT** beside the question. It scores 0. List NT topics in the score summary. |

Extra time, a reader or other arrangements for a known need are allowed: record them in the score summary.

#### Stopping rules

- The student may stop any section when they have done what they can. Say: "That is fine. Thank you. We will go on to the next part."
- **Listening:** if the student scores 2 or fewer out of 8 in Part A (questions 1 to 8), you may leave out Part B: check Part A quickly in the minute after it. Say: "Thank you. That is the end of the listening." Write "Part B not given" in the score summary; the band then comes from Part A alone (band A). In a group, read Part B to everyone as usual; a student with 2 or fewer in Part A may sit quietly.
- **Reading paper:** if the student cannot answer any of Part A after about 4 minutes, let them stop and record it. Do not move them on to Part B.
- **Writing:** if the student has written nothing in English after 3 minutes, say once: "Write anything you can in English: a few words or sentences about yourself is fine." Then let them stop when they wish.
- **Maths and science:** encourage the student to look at every page, because later questions on different topics may be easier for them. Do not stop early unless the student is distressed. Give the calculator when the student turns to Section C (page 16); in a group, give each student theirs when they reach that page. If a student goes back to Section A or B after that, take the calculator back while they work on it.
- If a student becomes upset at any point, stop, take a break, and continue only if they are happy to. Record it.

**Keeping the student at ease.** Smile, use the student's name, and keep your voice calm. Do not say "right" or "wrong" during the tasks; say "thank you" instead. Give a two-minute warning before the end of the reading paper, maths and science. A student who finishes early may check their work quietly.

## 3. English

### 3.1 Listening (16 marks: Part A 8, Part B 8)

There are two parts, and you read each one twice. **Part A** is the version 1 careers day talk, unchanged: everyday listening. **Part B** is a short lesson-style talk with subject vocabulary, statistics, a correction and an implied opinion: the listening that IGCSE lessons need, at the level of a fluent speaker of 14 to 15.

**Part A. Say:** "Turn to the Listening page. There are two parts. In Part A, you will hear a short talk by an engineer on a careers day at a school. I will read it two times. First, you have one minute to read questions 1 to 8." Wait one minute. **Say:** "Now listen." Read the Part A script. Wait 10 seconds. **Say:** "Now listen again." Read it again. Then give the student one more minute to finish.

**Part B. Say:** "Now turn to Part B, on page 4. You will hear a talk by a scientist, Professor Zhou Lan, about the climate of cities. I will read it two times. First, you have one minute to read questions 9 to 16." Wait one minute. **Say:** "Now listen." Read the Part B script. Wait 10 seconds. **Say:** "Now listen again." Read it again. Then give the student one more minute to finish.

**Read both scripts twice at a natural, steady pace** (about the speed of a teacher talking to a class, about 150 words a minute). At each [pause], stop for about 3 seconds. Do not slow down for key words, repeat sentences or explain anything. Read Part B at the same pace as Part A: it is meant to sound like a lesson.

**Timing.** Each reading of Part A takes about 2 minutes and each reading of Part B about 3.5 minutes, so the four readings take about 11 minutes. With one minute to read each set of questions, the two short gaps and one minute to finish each part, **allow about 15 minutes** for the listening, although the specification gives 10.

[[passage]]
#### Script, Part A: Careers Day talk

Good morning, everyone. My name is Dr Liu Yan, and I'm an engineer. I work for a company in Shenzhen that designs electric buses. Some of you probably came to school on one of our buses this morning. [pause]

When I was your age, I wasn't sure what I wanted to do. I was quite good at art, and I loved drawing. But my favourite subject was physics, because I always wanted to know how things worked. At the weekends, I took old radios apart and tried to put them back together. [pause]

After school, I studied engineering at university for four years. Then I worked in a battery factory for two years. Now I lead a team of twelve people. [pause] Our newest bus can travel three hundred kilometres before it needs charging, and it charges in about ninety minutes. [pause]

People often think engineers sit alone at a computer all day. In fact, I spend most of my time talking: to my team, to bus drivers, and to the people who use our buses. Last year, an older passenger told us the steps were too high. So we changed the design. Now the floor of the bus goes down when it stops. [pause]

My advice to you is simple. Don't be afraid of getting things wrong. I got a lot of things wrong with those old radios. My parents weren't always happy about that! [pause] My first battery design failed every test, too. But each time it failed, I learnt something new. And remember: being good at maths is useful, but being good at listening is just as important. Thank you. [pause]

[[passage]]
#### Script, Part B: a talk about cities and heat

Good afternoon, everyone. My name is Professor Zhou Lan, and I study the climate of cities. [pause]

On a still summer night, a thermometer in a city centre will almost always read higher than one in the fields outside. Scientists call this the urban heat island effect. "Urban" means "to do with cities", and a city really is an island of heat in the cooler countryside. At night, a city centre is about three degrees warmer. Actually, let me correct that: three degrees is only the average, and in the largest cities, on the hottest nights, the difference can reach seven degrees. [pause]

So why does this happen? First, roads, concrete and dark roofs absorb the sun's energy during the day and release it slowly after sunset, so the city never really cools down. Second, cities have far fewer trees and plants, which cool the air through a process called evapotranspiration. It's a long word for a simple idea: water travels up from the soil, evaporates from the leaves and carries heat away, rather like sweat cooling your skin. Third, there is waste heat from traffic and from air conditioners, which pump hot air out into the street. As a result, the hotter the city becomes, the more people switch their air conditioners on, and the hotter the streets get: a vicious circle. [pause]

One solution is the green roof: a roof covered with living plants and built in layers. At the bottom is a waterproof layer that protects the building. Above that is a drainage layer, which allows extra water to flow away. Then comes a thin layer of soil, and finally the plants: tough species that can survive for weeks without rain. [pause]

In 2023, my team tested two roofs side by side on a school in Hangzhou. On a July afternoon, the surface of the ordinary dark roof reached sixty-eight degrees, whereas the green roof stayed at around thirty-one. The classrooms under the green roof were also about four degrees cooler, so the school used noticeably less electricity for air conditioning. [pause]

Green roofs also soak up rainwater like a sponge, which reduces flooding, and they give insects and birds somewhere to live. On the other hand, they are heavier and cost more to build, and builders often tell me they are simply too expensive. I sometimes wonder how much they think a city that is too hot to live in will cost. [pause]

Painting roofs white also helps, because a white surface reflects sunlight rather than absorbing it. However, a white roof doesn't hold any rainwater, and it doesn't give a single butterfly anywhere to live. [pause]

Your sports hall has a large flat roof, and at the moment it is simply collecting heat. I'll leave you to think about what else it could be doing. Thank you. [pause]

[[/passage]]

**What is in the Part B script** (for the English team): 465 words in 28 sentences (16.6 words a sentence on average); about 14% of the words are outside the 2000 most common English words (about 20.5% counting each word form separately; `wordfreq` lists, names left out). It explains a process and a chain of cause and effect (three causes, the vicious circle, the layers of a green roof); compares the city with the countryside, a dark roof with a green roof, and a green roof with a white one; gives statistics and a date; explains subject vocabulary in context (urban, the urban heat island effect, evapotranspiration, a drainage layer); corrects a number ("Actually, let me correct that"); implies opinions without stating them (the builders, white roofs, the sports hall); and uses discourse markers (first, second, third, as a result, whereas, on the other hand, however).

#### Band guide (version 2)

| Result | Band |
|---|---|
| Part A 0 to 2 | **A** New to English |
| Part A 3 to 4 | **B** Early acquisition |
| Part A 5 to 6, or Part A 7 to 8 with Part B 0 to 2 | **C** Developing competence |
| Part A 7 to 8 with Part B 3 to 5 | **D** Competent |
| Part A 7 to 8 with Part B 6 to 8 | **E** Fluent |

If the two parts disagree in an unexpected way (for example Part A 5 or 6 with Part B 5 or more), use the interview to decide: the student may have lost easy marks in Part A through nerves or carelessness.

#### What to look for

- **Part A** checks everyday listening: questions 1 to 3 the main points, 4 to 6 exact details with distractors (two years, ninety minutes), and 7 and 8 implied meaning and the speaker's purpose.
- **Part B** is the step up. Questions 9 to 13 need exact detail from fast, connected speech, with distractors (3 degrees, 31 °C, the order of the layers); questions 14 to 16 need the student to join ideas, hear an attitude that is never stated and see the purpose of the whole talk. A student who gets 9 to 13 but not 14 to 16 follows the content of a lesson but not yet its argument.
- **Part A secure, Part B 0 to 2**: everyday English is fine, but lessons taught only by talk will be hard to follow at first. Plan visual support, key vocabulary in writing and frequent checks for understanding.
- Compare with listening in the interview. If the two differ by more than one band, use the interview as a check.

#### Band guide (version 3.1; use judgement when a result is uneven, and check against the sentence reading screen)

| Result | Band |
|---|---|
| Part A 0 to 3 | **A** New to English |
| Part A 4 or more, Part B 0 to 2 | **B** Early acquisition |
| Part B 3 to 5 | **C** Developing competence |
| Part B 6 to 8 | **D** Competent, or **E** Fluent (see below) |

**D or E.** Part B is the age-level text, so 6 or more shows at least a competent reader. Give **E** only when Part B is 7 or 8 **and** the sentence reading screen (meaning checks and fluency) and the writing also point to E. Version 3.1 has no text above age level, so the reading paper alone cannot confirm band E.

#### What to look for

- **Part B** (the version 1 article) is age-appropriate for a fluent reader of 14, and it is the hardest text on this paper. A Part B score of 7 or 8 shows a secure reader of age-level texts; whether the student reads like a fluent speaker has to come from the sentence reading screen and the writing as well (see the band guide).
- **Strong readers** in Part B retrieve exact details (8, 10 and 11), tell fact from opinion (12) and explain the writer's purpose in their own words rather than copying (13 and 14). Copying whole lines that are in the right place but do not answer the question is the commonest sign of a band D reader.
- **An English barrier** looks like: Part A secure, Part B patchy or copied lines. A student who reads aloud well (sentence reading) but scores low on Part B may be decoding without understanding: see the meaning checks on the sentence reading record.
- **Speed.** Note where the student was when time ran out. IGCSE papers are long; a student who is accurate but very slow will need reading practice and, possibly, an access arrangement assessment if there is a specific need.

### 3.3 Writing (10 minutes, 16 marks)

**Say:** "Turn to the Writing page. Choose one task, A or B, and tick the box. You can plan in the box, then write on the lines. You have 10 minutes. Write as much as you can." Do not explain the tasks or words.

**The tasks.** Task A (descriptive): describe a busy place (a market, a train station or a festival) so that the reader can see, hear and smell it. Task B (persuasive): an article for the school website on whether every student should volunteer for one week each year, with an opinion, reasons and examples. The plan is not marked.

**Score each criterion 0 to 4** (1 and 3 fall between the descriptions).

| Criterion | 0 | 2 | 4 (what a fluent 14 year old would do) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Content and organisation** | Nothing relevant in English, or a few words copied from the task. | Relevant to the task; ideas in a sensible order but little developed; may be one block of text; the description is a list of things, or the article lists reasons without explaining them. | Well developed and clearly organised in paragraphs. Description: a clear sense of place built up through the senses, a viewpoint or mood, a shape (for example moving through the place, or from morning to night). Article: a clear position, reasons developed with examples, some awareness of the other side, a conclusion; written for website readers. |
| **Vocabulary** | Isolated words, or only words from the prompt. | Everyday words used correctly but repeated ("many people", "very busy", "good"); a few words chosen for effect; simple linking (and, but, because). | Wide and precise vocabulary chosen for effect (sounds, smells, movement; persuasive phrases); a range of linking words (however, moreover, as a result, although); some figurative language that works. |
| **Grammar and sentences** | No sentences. | Mostly simple sentences, some joined with and, but, because; frequent errors in verb forms, plurals and articles, but the meaning is clear. | A variety of sentence types used for effect, including complex sentences and some short sentences for impact; verb forms controlled; errors are rare and do not affect meaning. |
| **Spelling and punctuation** | Mostly impossible to read. | Common words spelt correctly; capital letters and full stops mostly correct; errors in less common words. | Almost all words spelt correctly, including ambitious ones; a range of punctuation (commas in lists and after openers, question marks, apostrophes, colons) used correctly. |

**Band by best fit**, using the descriptors in `00_Admissions_Guide.md`, Section 6, and the notes below. The total is a check, not a rule: as a rough guide, totals of 0 to 3 usually go with A, 4 to 7 with B, 8 to 10 with C, 11 to 13 with D and 14 to 16 with E.

| Band | Writing at Year 10 entry |
|---|---|
| **A** | Name, isolated words or copied phrases; may write in Chinese. |
| **B** | A few short sentences on the topic, with frequent errors; meaning mostly clear. |
| **C** | A short connected piece (often 60 to 120 words) with some joined sentences; errors frequent but rarely block meaning; little paragraphing. |
| **D** | An organised piece (often 120 to 200 words) with paragraphs and a range of sentences; generally accurate with occasional errors; some varied vocabulary. |
| **E** | See below. |

**Band E writing at this age.** A fluent 14 year old usually writes 180 to 280 words in 10 minutes, in paragraphs, with controlled, varied sentences, precise vocabulary chosen for its effect on the reader, and accurate spelling and punctuation. The description creates a mood rather than listing objects; the article argues, gives evidence and examples, and speaks directly to its reader. This is roughly the level a First Language English IGCSE course starts from.

#### What to look for

- Typical features of English from speakers of Chinese (missing articles, plural -s, past tense -ed, third person -s) are normal at bands C and D. Do not let them alone pull a well-organised, well-developed piece below D.
- A memorised opening ("Nowadays, with the development of society") or memorised essay phrases followed by very simple writing shows exam training more than fluency: band by what the student can produce independently.
- For IGCSE, the gap between the writing band and the other bands matters: writing is assessed in almost every subject. If writing is two bands below reading, name it in the summary.

### 3.4 Interview, speaking and sentence reading (15 minutes, 1:1)

Use `forms/Interview_Record_Y7_Y10` for the interview and `shared/Sentence_Reading_Record` for the sentence reading. Both are in English.

- **Sentence reading first** (3 to 5 minutes). Year 10 starts at **Level 9 (sentence 25)**. Follow the start, going-back and stopping rules and the scoring on the record. ## 4. Maths (25 minutes, 30 marks)

**Say:** "Turn to the Maths page. You have 25 minutes. There are three sections. In Sections A and B, you may not use a calculator. When you get to Section C, on page 16, I will give you a calculator. Write your answers in the boxes and show your working. I can read any question to you in English. Do as much as you can, and look at every page."

The example box at the start of Section A shows what the command words and symbols mean (simplify fully, surd form, make ... the subject, f(*x*), fg(*x*), f<sup>−1</sup>(*x*)); the example at the start of Section B shows what "Write an equation, then solve it" means. They are there so that an English phrase or an unfamiliar symbol does not hide maths the student can do.

| Section | Questions | Marks | Calculator | What it measures |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| **B Word questions** | 7 to 10 | 8 | No | Maths given only in sentences, with no diagram: the student must read, decide what to do and, usually, form an equation. A reverse percentage, simultaneous equations from ticket prices, a quadratic from a rectangle, and combined rates. |

**Calculator.** Section C only. Before question 15, check that the calculator shows D or DEG (degrees). Many students from Chinese middle schools rarely use a calculator in maths lessons, so note how fluently they use one (powers, tan): IGCSE calculator papers need it.

**Marking.** M = method mark, A = accuracy mark (depends on the method), B = independent mark. A correct answer with no working gets full marks unless the scheme says otherwise: in questions 8 and 9 (which ask for an equation) a correct answer with no equation scores 1, and in questions 13, 15 and 16 a tick with no figures or reason scores 0. Units are not needed unless stated. Working in numbers and symbols is fine; any words must be in English (a reason written in Chinese scores 0, but the figures beside it still count).

**What a Year 10 entrant may not have been taught.** A student from a Chinese mainstream school has usually completed **Grade 8 (初二)**, so **algebra is usually a strength**: by then most have done factorising (因式分解), algebraic fractions and equations with fractions (分式, 分式方程), surds including rationalising the denominator (二次根式), negative integer indices, Pythagoras (勾股定理) and linear functions (一次函数, written *y* = *kx* + *b*). **Grade 9 (初三) topics may not have been taught yet**: quadratic equations and the formula (一元二次方程), probability (概率), similarity (相似) and trigonometry (锐角三角函数). Some early IGCSE content on this paper is taught in Chinese senior high school, or not at all, so it **may not have been taught** even to a strong student: fractional indices (分数指数幂), function notation with composite and inverse functions, volume scale factors, and upper and lower bounds. Use NT and judge whether the student would reach the expected band once taught: a strong Grade 8 student usually learns these quickly.

#### What to look for

- **Strong responses** show organised working, correct notation and decisions backed by figures (questions 13, 15 and 16), and attempt the Grade 9 and IGCSE topics even when they are new.
- **A correct tick with no reason** (questions 13, 15 and 16) is common from students used to answer-only questions. It scores 0, but it is a habit that can be taught quickly, not a gap in maths: note it.
- **English or maths?** Question 10 is maths that almost every Chinese-school student knows well (a work problem). If it is wrong or blank but the student solves it once it is read aloud, the barrier is English. The same check works for questions 7 to 9.
- **Weak responses** show errors on taught topics (questions 1, 3, 4, 7, 8, 10 and 11), not just on NT topics. If most of the lost marks are NT questions (2, 5, 6, 9, 12 to 16), write the likely band once taught in the summary.

## 5. Science (8 minutes, 10 marks)

**Say:** "Turn to the Science page. You have 8 minutes. I can read any question to you in English. Do as much as you can."

**What a Year 10 entrant may not have been taught.** In Chinese middle schools, biology runs from Grade 7, physics starts in Grade 8 (motion and speed, force, pressure, simple machines and efficiency) and chemistry only in Grade 9. A Year 10 entrant has usually done **biology and some physics but no chemistry**. Questions 6 and 7 may not have been taught: mark NT rather than reading this as weak science, and judge scientific thinking from question 4.

**Band guide** (provisional): 0 to 3: **1**; 4 to 5: **2**; 6 to 8: **3**; 9 to 10: **4**. Look at the NT questions before deciding.

**Not tested this year** (not enough time): diffusion, groups in the periodic table, rates of reaction, energy resources, waves, DNA and inheritance. Ask about them in the interview if they matter for subject choices.

#### What to look for

- **Working scientifically** (question 4) is the best guide to IGCSE science readiness: a student who describes a pattern, names a control variable and suggests a real improvement is ready for practical work in English, even with chemistry NT.
- **An English barrier** looks like: blank or wrong answers that become right when you read the question aloud. Science vocabulary in English (specialised, efficiency, variable, mass number) is often the gap: note the words the student did not know.
- **Strong responses** use units (m/s, N/m²) and give precise answers in question 4 (for example "repeat three times and calculate a mean").

## 6. Readiness for IGCSE

**This section is guidance for the school to confirm**, against its own admissions policy, the IGCSE syllabuses it offers (for example Cambridge IGCSE or Pearson Edexcel International GCSE), the entry requirements of its own sixth form or diploma programme, and the support it can provide. It is not a set of fixed rules. Fill in the score summary (Section 7) first, then read the results together.

**English is the gateway.** Every IGCSE subject except other languages is taught and examined in English, with long texts, extended written answers and command words such as describe, explain and evaluate. Look at the overall English band, and most closely at **reading and writing**, because the exams depend on them. Academic English usually develops more slowly than conversation: as a planning assumption, a student rarely moves up more than about one band a year in academic reading and writing, even with good support. A student at band C at the start of Year 10 may still be at C or D when the exams come.

| English band | What it usually means for IGCSE study in English | English course that may fit best |
|---|---|---|
| **A** New to English | Cannot yet follow subject lessons in English. Direct entry to a two-year IGCSE course is not usually advisable (`00_Admissions_Guide.md`, Section 9). | A preparatory or foundation route, or a later start, if the school has one. |
| **B** Early acquisition | Can follow simple, supported lessons, but IGCSE texts and exam questions will be very hard for most of Year 10. | Only with an English as a Second Language course, a reduced subject load and close review, if the school's policy allows. |
| **C** Developing competence | Can follow most lessons with support; subject vocabulary and extended writing will slow progress. Maths and the sciences are usually more accessible than essay-based subjects. | **English as a Second Language**, with support for subject language. |
| **D** Competent | Usually ready for IGCSE courses taught in English, with some support for academic writing. | English as a Second Language at the higher level, or First Language English if reading is band E **and** writing is a strong D or E (see below). Check with the English department. |
| **E** Fluent | Ready for IGCSE study in English. | **First Language English** (and English Literature, if offered). |

**What English band does a student realistically need?** As a working guide for the school to test: **band C or above in reading and writing**, with listening and speaking at C or above, to follow IGCSE courses taught in English with support, and **band D or above** to follow them with little extra support. Review these thresholds once the first groups of students have taken their IGCSEs.

**What the version 3.1 English results add.** The listening can tell a competent student (D) from a fluent one (E); the reading paper has no text above age level, so it can show a competent reader but cannot on its own confirm a fluent one. Use the hardest parts as the evidence for IGCSE:

- **Listening Part B** is the best guide to following lessons taught by talk. Part B 6 or more: the student can follow a lesson-style explanation with subject vocabulary. Part B 0 to 2 with Part A secure: everyday listening is fine, but lessons will need visual support and key vocabulary in writing at first.
- **Reading Part B** ("Ten Minutes of Nothing") is the age-level text and the hardest reading on this paper. Part B 7 or 8, with writing at E or a strong D: close to the reading of a fluent speaker. Part B 6 or more otherwise: a competent reader of age-level texts, who will need support with the longest IGCSE texts. Because version 3.1 has no text above age level, when First Language English is being considered, confirm it with the English department (for example with a further reading task).

**English as a Second Language or First Language English?** First Language English assumes the reading and writing of a fluent speaker: analysing how writers use language, and writing at length for different purposes. It is usually the better fit only when reading is band E (Part B 7 or 8, with the sentence reading screen also pointing to E: see 3.2) and writing is band E or a strong D. Because version 3.1 has no reading text above age level, confirm the reading with the English department first. For most students at band C or D, an English as a Second Language course is the better fit: it teaches the reading, writing, listening and speaking needed for study, and a good grade is more achievable. Check what the school's sixth form, diploma programme and likely universities ask for, because some ask for a particular course or grade; tell the family early.

**Maths: Extended or Core tier, and what support.** Read the total band with the three sub-scores (Section 4), allowing for NT.
