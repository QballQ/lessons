#### Ten Minutes of Nothing

*By Zhang Yiran, Year 11*

When did you last do nothing at all? I do not mean sleeping, and I do not mean scrolling through videos. I mean sitting still, with no screen, no music and no plan, and simply letting your mind wander.

If you are like most teenagers I know, you probably cannot remember. The moment we feel even slightly bored (in a queue, on the bus, while a video is loading) our hands reach for our phones. Boredom feels like a problem, and our phones offer an instant solution.

I would like to suggest that boredom is not a problem at all. In fact, it may be one of the most useful feelings we have.

Psychologists describe boredom as a kind of signal. It tells us that what we are doing is not satisfying, and it pushes us to look for something better. When we fill every empty moment with a screen, we never hear that signal. We stay busy, but we are not really choosing what to do with our time.

There is also evidence that a wandering mind can be a creative one. In one study, a group of adults spent fifteen minutes copying numbers from a long list. Afterwards, they were asked to think of as many uses as possible for two plastic cups. They came up with more ideas than a group who had skipped the boring task. The researchers believe that boredom gives the mind time to make unexpected connections.

Of course, nobody enjoys being bored for hours, and I am not suggesting that we throw our phones into the nearest river. My suggestion is much smaller: try ten minutes of nothing each day. Leave your phone in your bag on the way home. Look out of the window. Notice where your thoughts go.

The first few minutes will feel strange, perhaps even uncomfortable. But you may be surprised by what your mind does when you finally give it some space.
[[/passage]]

[[passage]]
#### Can This Be Saved?

*By Lin Qiao*

<span class="pn">1</span> My desk lamp died on a Tuesday evening, in the middle of a sentence I was rather proud of. It flickered twice, buzzed indignantly, and went dark. My father glanced in from the doorway. "Just order another one," he said. "They're cheap."

<span class="pn">2</span> He was right, of course. A new lamp would have cost less than a cinema ticket and arrived the next morning. Instead, I carried it to a repair café in an old community hall, where a retired electrician called Mr Fang peered into its base, tutted, and replaced a small, blackened switch. The whole operation took eleven minutes. The lamp is still on my desk, and I am still faintly ridiculous about how pleased I am.

<span class="pn">3</span> I tell this story because it seems to me that, somewhere along the way, we quietly stopped believing that things can be fixed. When a zip breaks, we buy a new jacket. When a screen cracks, we upgrade. Our cupboards fill with objects that work perfectly well apart from one small, maddening fault, and eventually they make their way to a landfill site, where they will outlast every one of us. Without quite noticing, we have become a society of replacers.

<span class="pn">4</span> Some of this is not our fault. Many products are now designed in ways that make repair almost impossible: cases are glued rather than screwed together, spare parts are never sold, and the instruction manual has been replaced by a cheerful warning that opening the device will cancel your guarantee. Manufacturers insist that this is about safety and elegant design. Perhaps. It is certainly also about selling us the same object twice.

<span class="pn">5</span> Nevertheless, it would be too easy to blame the companies alone. The deeper problem, I suspect, is a habit of mind. We have learnt to treat newness as a kind of virtue, and anything old as faintly embarrassing. A patched elbow or a re-glued chair leg, which my grandmother would have regarded as a small triumph, now looks to many people like a sign of poverty or neglect. She darned socks until they were more darn than sock, and she would have been baffled by the idea of throwing away anything that could still be coaxed back to life.

<span class="pn">6</span> I would argue the opposite. A mended object carries a story. The tiny scratch on my lamp, where Mr Fang's screwdriver slipped, reminds me of our conversation about the factory where he trained, and of his dry remark that "nothing is broken until you stop being curious about it". You do not get that from a delivery van: a new lamp, however elegant, arrives with no history at all.

<span class="pn">7</span> There is a practical argument, too. Every object we keep for longer is one fewer that has to be mined, manufactured, packaged and shipped across the world. Repairing a lamp will not save the planet, and I am not naive enough to pretend otherwise. But habits are contagious. The volunteers at the repair café told me that people who bring one broken thing usually return with another, and then with a neighbour.

<span class="pn">8</span> Critics will say that repair is a luxury for those with time to spare, and they have a point. Not everyone can give up a Saturday afternoon to watch a stranger rewire a toaster. That is precisely why repair should not depend on the goodwill of retired electricians. Schools could teach basic mending alongside cooking and computing. Shops could be required to stock spare parts for as long as they sell a product. Cities could fund repair cafés in the same way that they fund libraries: as places where knowledge is shared rather than sold.

<span class="pn">9</span> None of this means giving up new things altogether. It simply asks us to pause before we throw something away, and to ask a question that earlier generations asked without thinking: can this be saved?

<span class="pn">10</span> My lamp, for the record, is shining as I write. It is not the newest lamp in the world, nor the most beautiful. But it is mine, it works, and every time I switch it on, it reminds me that broken is not the same as finished.
[[/passage]]
