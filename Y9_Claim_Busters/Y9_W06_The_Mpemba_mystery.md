## 1. Lesson plan (one page, for the teacher)

- **Big idea:** Even a simple-sounding claim can be hard to test: scientists must agree exactly what they will measure (an operational definition), and different definitions, or different conditions, can give different answers.
- **Learning goals:**
  - I can carry out a fair test of the claim "Hot water freezes faster than cold water" (Plan A) or compare the cooling of hot and warm water (Plan B), and record my results.
  - I can explain what an operational definition is, show how the choice of definition can change a verdict, and say what my evidence can and cannot show.

**Key words (10)**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Mpemba effect | 姆潘巴效应 | The claim that hot water can freeze faster than cold water. |
| operational definition | 操作性定义 | Saying exactly what we will measure or observe, so everyone counts the same thing. |
| freezing point | 凝固点 | The temperature at which a liquid turns into a solid. For pure water it is 0 °C. |
| freezing mixture | 冷冻混合物 | Ice mixed with salt. It gets much colder than 0 °C. |
| supercooling | 过冷 | When water cools below 0 °C but has not turned to ice yet. |
| cooling curve | 冷却曲线 | A line graph of temperature against time as something cools. |
| rate of cooling | 冷却速率 | How fast the temperature falls, in °C per minute. |
| contested | 有争议的 | Scientists still disagree about it. |
| reproducible | 可重复的 | Other people get the same result when they repeat the test. |
| limitation | 局限性 | Something that stops our evidence from showing everything. |

**Timeline (Plan B):** the same as Plan A except:
- **Learn:** the teacher pours about 100 ml of water at about 60 °C into each group's "H" cup and about 100 ml at about 35 °C into each "W" cup (about 2 minutes for 6 groups). Groups start the stopwatch when both cups are filled and read both thermometers every minute. The definitions part teaches "What counts as cooling faster?" (slide 9B).
- **Do:** readings continue every minute to 15 minutes (about minute 26 of the lesson). Students plot both cooling curves as they go, then add their 15-minute drops to the class table.
- **Think and Reflect:** the debate is "Does cooling faster at first prove freezing first?" and the limits of evidence (slides 12B and 13).

**Tiers**
- **Core:** record the group's results in the table; complete the class tally (Plan A) or plot both cooling curves (Plan B); gap fill on the key words.
- **Stretch:** Plan A: compare class means and ranges under each definition and say which definition gives hot water its best chance. Plan B: calculate the rate of cooling for the first 5 minutes and the total drop for each cup, and say which cup is colder at 15 minutes.
- **Challenge:** Plan A: use the readings to explain supercooling and design a fairer test. Plan B: extend the hot curve to predict when it reaches 35 °C and explain why cooling faster at first does not prove freezing first. Both: explain why Mpemba's ice-cream observation was not enough to prove the claim for water.

**Language support**
- Frames: "Our operational definition of frozen is ___." / "Using this definition, the ___ water froze first." / "The data suggests ___; however, ___." / "Our evidence cannot show ___ because ___." / "Our verdict is ___ because ___."
- Chinese talk is welcome in the Hook vote, during predictions, and in the definition debate. Readings are called out in English ("minus two degrees", "two point five minutes").
- Vocabulary traps: "frozen" in everyday speech means "very cold" ("I'm frozen!"); here we must define it. "Cool" can mean "lower in temperature" or "good". "Freezing point" is a temperature, not a moment in time. Say negative temperatures as "minus three degrees". 凝固 and 结冰 are both used for "freeze"; both are fine.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- "Hot water must freeze first because it cools faster." It cools faster at first because it is further above the surroundings, but it also has further to go.
- "Water always freezes at exactly 0 °C." Water can supercool to a few degrees below 0 °C before the first crystals form, and this can vary from tube to tube.
- "If our test gave one answer, the question is settled." One set of conditions, one definition and a few tubes cannot settle a contested question.
- "Scientists disagreeing means science is broken." Disagreement plus more careful testing is how science works.

**Safety**
- Plan A: the ice and salt freezing mixture reaches about minus 10 to minus 18 °C and can hurt skin (cold burn). Students wear gloves, handle the tubes by the top, and never put fingers into the mixture. Goggles on while the mixture is on the table (salty splashes sting eyes). Glass thermometers are never used as stirrers and never pulled out of ice.
- Both plans: warm water is prepared and poured by the teacher only, no hotter than about 60 °C for student handling. Cups and tubes of warm water stay on the tray. Spills are wiped up at once.
- No eating or drinking; nothing used in the practical is tasted, including ice and salt.
- Full bilingual lines are on slides 6A and 6B and on the kit card.

**If time is short:** Plan A: drop the "first crystals" checks and keep "reaches 0 °C" and "fully solid"; if tubes are not fully solid by minute 30, record "not solid at ___ min" (that is still a result). Use the class sample data from the answer key if the board table is not complete. Plan B: stop readings at 10 minutes and plot to 10. Both: cut Challenge; keep the definition debate and the verdict, which are the point of the lesson.

**Assessment and handover links**
- Output: class results and the definition debate (Plan A), or two cooling curves and the limits-of-evidence debate (Plan B).
- Exit ticket: operational definition, two definitions of "frozen", and a verdict with a reason.
- Logbook line: What I found 我发现了什么: "The answer changed when we changed the definition of ___." (Plan A) or "Hot water cooled faster, but ___." (Plan B).
- Tracker: record each student's project group (Weeks 6 to 10) in column F.
- Link to Week 7: the last slide previews the claim bank. Groups choose their own claim next week, and claim 1 (this one) is on the list in Plan A.

Model: "Hot water did best under the definition 'first crystals', because the means were close (4.8 and 4.2 minutes), the ranges overlapped a lot, and in one group the hot tube got crystals first. Under 'reaches 0 °C' cold water won in every group."

**Stretch 2 (Plan A, Secure model):** "The data suggests cold water freezes first in our conditions, because it reached 0 °C first in all six groups and was fully solid first in five; however, the 'first crystals' results were mixed and the ranges overlapped. Our evidence cannot show what happens in a real freezer, with bigger amounts of water or other starting temperatures, because we only tested one set of conditions."

**Challenge 1 (Plan A model):** "The water was supercooled: it went below 0 °C but stayed liquid because ice crystals had not started to form. When the first crystal formed, ice spread quickly and released heat, so the temperature jumped back up to 0 °C. Supercooling is unpredictable (it depends on tiny bits of dust, scratches or bumps), so the time of the first crystals changes from tube to tube even when everything else is the same. That makes 'first crystals' hard to reproduce."

**Challenge 2 (Plan A model):** "I would choose 'fully solid' (tilt the tube, nothing moves) and write it down before starting. I would use at least three hot and three cold tubes, all the same type, with the same volume of water (3 ml), in the same freezing mixture at the same time, checked at the same interval by the same person." Excellent: mentions measuring the bath temperature, or that the person checking should not know which tube is which (blind).

**Core 2 (graph):** two smooth curves that do not cross: H falls steeply at first and then more gently, from 60 °C to 40 °C; W falls gently from 35 °C to 29 °C. Points within one small square.

**Prediction check (Plan B):** bigger drop in 15 min: H. Faster rate in first 5 min: H. Reaches 30 °C first: W (at about 11 minutes; H does not reach it in 15 minutes).

**Stretch 1 (Plan B, sample):** hot: (60.0 minus 52.0) ÷ 5 = 1.6 °C per minute; warm: (35.0 minus 32.5) ÷ 5 = 0.5 °C per minute. "The hot cup cooled faster at first. At 15 minutes, the warm cup was colder."

**Stretch 2 (Plan B, Secure model):** "The data suggests hot water cools faster than warm water, more than three times as fast in the first 5 minutes; however, after 15 minutes the hot cup was still 11 °C warmer than the warm cup. Our evidence cannot show which would freeze first because the water never got near 0 °C."

**Challenge 1 (Plan B):** about 20 to 25 minutes (the sample curve reaches 35 °C at about 21 to 22 minutes). By then the warm cup was about 28 °C (accept 27 to 29 °C).

**Challenge 2 (Plan B model):** "Cooling faster is not the same as getting to 0 °C first. The hot cup started 25 °C higher, and it cools more slowly as it gets closer to room temperature, so in our data it never caught up. To prove it freezes first we would need to actually freeze both, with an agreed definition of frozen, and repeat it many times." Excellent: names a way the hot water could overtake, for example losing mass by evaporation, or the cold water supercooling for longer.

**Challenge 3 (both, model, any two):** it was ice-cream mixture, not water (it contains milk, fat and sugar); it was one observation, not repeated; the bowls may not have been the same size or in the same place in the freezer; "seemed to freeze" was not defined (no operational definition); nobody measured the temperatures; a hot bowl may have melted frost and sat more firmly on the freezer shelf.

**My thinking (model):** "Because the answer depends on the conditions (container, amount of water, starting temperatures, the freezer) and on what you count as frozen. Supercooling is random, so results change from one try to the next, and different labs get different answers. When results are hard to reproduce, scientists keep disagreeing until someone finds a test everyone trusts."

**Exit ticket:** 1. Saying exactly what you will measure or observe, so everyone counts the same thing. 2. Any two of: reaches 0 °C; first ice crystals appear; fully solid (nothing moves when tilted); a set temperature such as minus 5 °C. 3. Plan A: usually Not supported for our test (accept Not enough evidence yet with a reason about overlapping results or a small number of tubes). Plan B: Not enough evidence yet, because we did not freeze the water (Not supported is not justified by cooling data alone).

**Concept cartoon (slide 12A):** both students can be honest. Mei used "reaches 0 °C"; Jun used "first crystals". The fault is that they did not agree a definition before testing. Tao is right.

- Insulated cup (polystyrene, or two plastic cups one inside the other) of freezing mixture, about 300 g crushed ice and 100 g salt: 1 (made by the teacher)
- Small test tubes, each marked with a line at 3 ml and labelled H1, C1, H2, C2: 4
- Thermometers (minus 10 to 110 °C): 2
- Stopwatch: 1
- Cup of cool tap water (about 20 °C) and a 10 ml plastic syringe or small measuring cylinder: 1 each
- Disposable gloves: 2 pairs (Measurer and Safety Officer), plus spares on the teacher's desk
- Goggles: 4
- Tray, paper towels, tube rack or empty cup to stand tubes in: 1 each

**Plan B: per group (6 groups of 4)**
- Identical plastic cups (about 200 ml), labelled H and W: 2
- Thermometers (minus 10 to 110 °C): 2
- Stopwatch: 1
- Tray and paper towels: 1 each

**If you have only 6 thermometers (both plans):** each group measures one tube or cup only. Odd-numbered groups do H, even-numbered groups do W or C, and pairs of groups compare.

**Teacher**
- Kettle, large jug, a flask to keep water at about 60 °C, a spare thermometer to check it
- Plan A: about 2 to 3 kg of ice in an insulated box, 1 kg of salt, a spoon, a 10 ml syringe for filling H tubes, a bucket for used mixture
- Plan B: a second jug for water at about 35 °C, a 100 ml measuring jug

**Teacher prep before the lesson**
- Plan A (about 25 minutes): collect the ice (crush it in a clean bag with a rolling pin if it is in large cubes); mark and label the tubes; 10 minutes before the lesson, mix about 3 parts ice to 1 part salt by mass in each insulated cup with a splash of cold water, stir and check that it is below about minus 10 °C. Make warm water at about 60 °C (kettle water mixed with cold water, checked with a thermometer) and keep it in the flask.
- Plan B (about 10 minutes): mix two jugs of water, one at about 60 °C and one at about 35 °C, checked with a thermometer, just before the lesson.

**Hazards and controls**
- The ice and salt mixture is cold enough to hurt skin. Wear gloves. Handle the tubes by the top, never the mixture.
- 冰盐混合物温度很低，会冻伤皮肤。请戴手套。只拿试管上端，不要接触混合物。
- Salty splashes sting eyes. Wear goggles while the mixture is on the table, and wash your hands afterwards.
- 盐水溅入眼睛会刺痛。冷冻混合物在桌上时请戴护目镜，实验后请洗手。
- Warm water can scald. Only the teacher prepares and pours warm water, no hotter than about 60 °C. Do not move or carry cups of warm water.
- 温水可能烫伤。只有老师可以准备和倒温水，温度不超过约60 °C。不要移动或端起装有温水的杯子。
- Glass thermometers break easily. Never stir with a thermometer or pull one out of ice. If one breaks, step back and tell the teacher.
- 玻璃温度计容易破碎。不要用温度计搅拌，也不要把温度计从冰中硬拔出来。如果温度计破了，请后退并告诉老师。
- No eating or drinking. Never taste the ice, salt or water.
- 禁止饮食。不要品尝冰、盐或水。
- Spills make floors slippery. Wipe up spills at once.
- 洒出的水会使地面湿滑。洒出的水要立即擦干。

**Clean-up**
- Plan A: tip used mixture into the bucket; the teacher pours it down the sink with plenty of running water. Let frozen tubes thaw in the rack before washing. Count thermometers (12) and tubes (24).
- Plan B: the teacher empties the cups. Count thermometers.
- Wipe trays and tables; wash hands.
