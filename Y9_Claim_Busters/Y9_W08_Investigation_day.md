## 1. Lesson plan (one page, for the teacher)

- **Big idea:** A verdict is only as good as the data behind it, so scientists follow their plan, record raw data straight away, repeat, and note every anomaly and change honestly.
- **Learning goals:**
  - I can carry out our approved plan safely and record raw data with units, repeats and notes.
  - I can check the quality of our data, spot anomalies and explain how we handled them.

**Key words (9)**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| raw data | 原始数据 | The measurements exactly as we took them, before any calculation. |
| data quality | 数据质量 | How much we can trust our data. |
| consistent | 一致的 | Done the same way every time. |
| repeat | 重复 | Do the same test again to check. |
| anomaly | 异常值 | A result that does not fit the pattern. |
| resolution | 分辨率 | The smallest change a measuring tool can show. |
| uncertainty | 不确定性 | How unsure we are about a result, because measurements vary. |
| mean | 平均值 | Add up all the values, then divide by how many there are. |
| range | 极差 | The biggest value minus the smallest value. |

**Timeline**

| Phase | Min | Teacher does | Students do | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do Now | 4 | Has every group's tray on its table before students arrive (built from the approval stubs). Hands back the approved plans. | Kit check: Safety Officer ticks the tray against the plan's kit list; Lead Scientist reads the method aloud; Recorder copies the results-table headings onto the data sheet. | Slides 1 to 2, trays, plans, data sheet |
| Hook | 3 | Shows two data sheets for the same test, one careful and one messy. "Which would a scientist trust? Why?" | Pair talk, then share one reason with a frame. | Slide 3 |
| Learn | 7 | Three data-quality rules (2 min); anomalies and resolution (2 min); reads the safety slide in both languages (1 min). Start signal (2 min): pours warm water for claims 1, 3 and 4 (in that order); claim 10 opens bottles; ice claims take their ice from the cool box. | Listen, then set up and start the moment the teacher says "Go". | Slides 4 to 8, run sheet |
| Do | 18 | Runs the clock from the run sheet: volunteer window opens at minute 16 and closes at minute 28; halfway check at minute 23 ("At least two repeats of each condition?"). Circulates: safety first, then consistency and recording. | Carry out the plan in roles; record raw data in pen; human-participant groups test volunteers; Stretch on the worksheet while waiting for readings. | Slides 9 to 10, data sheet, worksheet |
| Think and Reflect | 8 | Calls "Stop" at minute 32. Clean-up (3 min). Photographs or collects every data sheet (2 min, during the checklist). Collects exit tickets (2 min). Logbook line (1 min). | Clean up with the kit card; complete the data-quality checklist as a group; exit ticket; logbook line. | Slides 11 to 12, data-quality checklist, worksheet |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** do your role; record raw data with units; complete the data-quality checklist; gap fill on the key words.
- **Stretch:** calculate the mean and range for each condition and describe the early pattern with "The data suggests ___; however, ___."
- **Challenge:** explain how an anomaly was handled; compare the size of the difference with the resolution of the measuring tool; explain how a change to the plan could affect the verdict.

**Language support**
- Frames: "We noticed an anomaly in ___. We think it happened because ___. We ___ (repeated it / kept it and noted it)." / "We changed our plan because ___." / "The data suggests ___; however, ___." / "Would you like to take part in a short test? You can say no or stop at any time."
- Chinese talk is welcome while working; readings are called out in English numbers ("thirty-four point five degrees").
- Vocabulary traps: "raw" does not mean uncooked here; it means "not yet calculated". "Resolution" is not a New Year's resolution. "Consistent" is not "constant". 异常值 is an unusual result, not a mistake: it is checked, not deleted.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- "An anomaly is a mistake, so we rub it out." Circle it, note it, repeat if possible; never hide it.
- "We changed the plan, so our results are ruined." Sensible changes are fine if they are written down with a reason (rubric strand 3, Excellent).
- "More decimal places means better data." Write what the tool can actually show (its resolution).
- "If the result is what we predicted, the data must be good." Good data is about method, not about the answer.

**Safety:** this lesson has the most hazards of the series because groups do different practicals at once. Check each group's risk table before they start. Main hazards: ice and salt mixtures (gloves, handle tubes and cups, never the mixture); warm water poured by the teacher only, no hotter than about 60 °C (use about 50 °C for claims 3 and 4); no eating or drinking (fizzy drinks are never tasted); glass thermometers (never used to stir); spills (wiped up at once); tape measures and rulers; human-participant ethics. Full bilingual lines are on slide 7 and the kit card.

**If time is short:** 2 repeats of each condition is the minimum (3 the target). Cooling claims can read at 0, 5 and 10 minutes instead of every minute. Human-participant groups stop at the number of volunteers they have at minute 28 and say so in their limitations. Never cut clean-up or the data-quality checklist.

**Assessment and handover links**
- Output: one data sheet per group (raw data, notes, changes), photographed or collected before students leave. This is the main evidence for rubric strand 3 (Doing and recording).
- Teacher notes during the lesson: on your copy of the run sheet, jot one line per group on who did what and how carefully (supports individual strand 3 levels).
- Exit ticket: repeats achieved, one data-quality action, one problem.
- Logbook line: What I found 我发现了什么: "Our first results suggest ___."
- Keep data sheets safe: groups need them in Week 9 to write the fact-check report.

## 4. Answer key (for the teacher)

**Core 1:** depends on the group. Resolution examples: thermometer 1 °C marks, read to 0.5 °C; kitchen scale 1 g or 0.1 g; ruler 1 mm (0.1 cm); stopwatch 0.01 s (but human timing is only good to about 0.2 s, a good Challenge point).

**Core 2:** a) raw b) consistent c) anomaly d) resolution e) repeat

**Stretch 1 and 2 (model, claim 4):** wet cloth drops 12.5, 13.0, 11.5 °C: mean 37.0 ÷ 3 = 12.3 °C, range 1.5 °C. Dry cloth drops 7.0, 6.5, 7.5 °C: mean 21.0 ÷ 3 = 7.0 °C, range 1.0 °C. "So far, the data suggests that wet cloth makes the water cool faster, because the mean drop was 5.3 °C bigger and the ranges did not overlap; however, we only tested three cups of each in one room."

**Challenge 1 (model):** "The result 9.0 °C for wet cup 2 did not fit because the other two were about 12 °C. It might have happened because the cloth had dried at the top. We wetted it again and repeated that cup: 11.5 °C. We kept the first value on the sheet, circled, with a note."

**Challenge 2 (model):** "The difference between our means was 5.3 °C. The resolution was 0.5 °C. The difference is ten times the resolution and bigger than both ranges, so we can be fairly confident it is real. (If the difference were 0.5 °C, about the same as the resolution, we could not be confident at all.)" For claim 10 with a 1 g scale: "Our difference was 0 g, but the scale cannot show less than 1 g, so we cannot tell: not enough evidence yet."

**Challenge 3 (model):** "We changed from reading every minute to reading at 0, 5 and 10 minutes, because one thermometer broke and we had to share. This should not change the drop in 10 minutes, but readings were about 30 seconds apart between cups, so the times were not exactly the same: that adds some uncertainty."

**My thinking (model):** "A surprise means the world is different from what we expected, so we might learn something new. It is also a warning to check the method, but if the method was good, a surprise is a discovery."

**Exit ticket:** any honest answer; 3 repeats or more is the target, 2 the minimum. Look for specific data-quality actions (units, pen, consistent method, anomaly repeated, codes for people).

### Teacher notes for each claim: what results usually show

Use these to circulate, to ask good questions, and to check that groups' data is plausible. Do not give the expected result to students before they collect data; use it afterwards in Week 9 conversations. Typical values are rough and will vary with room temperature and kit.

| Claim | What usually happens | Why (the science) | Usual verdict | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Hot water freezes faster** | As in Week 6: cold water reaches 0 °C first in almost every tube; "fully solid" usually goes to cold water by 1 to 2 minutes; "first crystals" is mixed. | Hot water has further to cool. Supercooling is random, so the moment the first crystals form varies from tube to tube. | Not supported for their conditions; Not enough evidence yet for the claim in general (still contested). | The definition must be fixed before testing. The freezing mixture warms up over time: tubes must go in together. |
| **2. Ice melts faster in salt water** | The surprise: ice melts **faster in fresh water**. A cube in 200 ml of room-temperature fresh water often melts in about 6 to 10 minutes; in salt water (about 1 tablespoon of salt in 200 ml) it can take about twice as long. | In fresh water, the cold meltwater is denser than the warmer water around it, so it sinks and warmer water flows in to touch the ice. In salt water, the fresh meltwater is less dense than the salty water, so it floats around the ice as a cold, insulating layer. Food colouring in the ice shows this beautifully: it sinks in fresh water and spreads across the top in salt water. | Not supported. | Water in both cups must start at the same temperature. Stirring destroys the effect (and is a different question). Groups often expect the opposite because of claim 5. |
| **3. Most body heat lost through the head** | A bottle with fabric on the top cools almost the same as an uncovered bottle; the same fabric on the side has a similar small effect. Wrapping the whole bottle makes a big difference. | Heat loss from any surface depends on its area. The head is roughly 10% of the body's surface, so it loses roughly that share of heat when everything is uncovered, not "most". The myth is often traced to an old army test in which volunteers wore warm suits but no hats, so the head was the only uncovered part. The head and face also feel the cold strongly. | "You lose most of your body heat through your head": Not supported. "A hat keeps you warm": partly supported (covering any uncovered skin reduces heat loss, so a hat helps if the rest of you is covered). | Groups must split the two-part claim. Bottles need the same volume and starting temperature (about 50 °C, poured by the teacher). Differences under 1 °C are within the uncertainty. |
| **4. Wet clothes make you lose heat faster** | From about 50 °C, over 10 minutes: dry-cloth cups drop about 5 to 7 °C; wet-cloth cups drop about 9 to 13 °C. | Water evaporating from the cloth takes heat away (evaporative cooling), and wet cloth conducts heat better than dry cloth, which traps air. | Supported. | The cloth must be wetted with room-temperature water; cold water adds a confounding variable. Cloth must be equally wet each time (squeeze the same way). |
| **5. Salt on ice makes it melt faster** | After 10 minutes at room temperature, a salted cube usually loses more mass than an unsalted one (for a cube of about 15 g, perhaps 6 to 8 g lost against 3 to 5 g). The salty puddle feels colder and can go below 0 °C. | Salt lowers the freezing point of water, so salty water stays liquid below 0 °C and ice in contact with it keeps melting even at temperatures just below 0 °C. That is why roads are salted in winter (it works well just below 0 °C, but much less well in very cold weather). Melting takes in heat, which is why the mixture gets colder: the same idea as the Week 6 freezing mixture. | Supported. | Weigh the remaining ice (patted dry), not the puddle, because dissolved salt adds mass to the puddle. Cubes must start the same size (weigh them first). Gloves on: salted ice is very cold. |
| **6. Crushed ice cools a drink faster** | 200 ml of water at about 20 °C with 50 g of crushed ice often falls to about 3 to 6 °C within 2 minutes; with 50 g of cubes it is usually about 8 to 12 °C after 2 minutes. After 5 minutes the two cups are often within a few degrees. | Crushed ice has a much greater surface area, so heat flows from the water into the ice faster. The same mass of ice can absorb the same total amount of heat, so the final temperatures end up close. | Supported (for "faster"); a good Excellent point is that the final temperature is similar. | Same mass of ice, weighed. Stir both the same way, or neither. Tap water only; never a real drink. |
| **7. Music helps memory** | Usually a small difference (often within 1 or 2 words out of 20), with overlapping ranges. Music with lyrics sometimes gives slightly lower scores. | Some people find background music distracting, especially with words; others feel calmer. Individual differences are large, so small samples cannot settle it. | Not enough evidence yet (sometimes Not supported). | Two different word lists of equal difficulty (6e); half the participants do music first (counterbalancing). Same timings as Week 5. One device: test one or two people at a time. |
| **8. Practice makes you faster** | Times drop over five tries, steeply at first and then levelling off (for example 45, 36, 32, 30, 29 seconds). | Learning curve: the brain gets faster with practice. On the same sheet, people also remember where the numbers are. | Supported (for this puzzle). | Is it skill or memory? Challenge groups can test layout B after five tries on layout A. Same instructions every time; the same person times. |
| **9. Taller people have bigger feet / arm span equals height** | Foot length and height show a positive correlation, but with many exceptions (foot length is often about 15% of height). Arm span is usually within about 5 cm of height (see Week 4 sample data). | Body proportions are similar on average but vary from person to person. | "Taller people tend to have bigger feet": Supported as a trend, not for every person. "Arm span equals height": Supported as roughly equal, Not supported as exactly equal (operational definition matters). | Voluntary measuring, codes only, no comments on size. Feet measured standing on paper with socks on: mark heel and longest toe, measure the line. Same rule for shoes for height. |
| **10. Spoon in a fizzy drink keeps it fizzy** | No measurable difference. In 20 minutes an open, unshaken small bottle typically loses well under 1 g; with and without a spoon the losses are within 0.1 to 0.2 g of each other, inside the range. | A spoon in the neck does not seal the bottle or stop gas escaping from the drink's surface. Careful tests by others have also found no effect. | Not supported (or Not enough evidence yet if the scale only reads to 1 g). | Needs a 0.1 g scale. Bottles at the same temperature (out of the fridge since the morning), opened at the same moment, never shaken, never drunk. Pour away at the end. |
| **11. Brand X paper towel is the most absorbent** | Brands often differ clearly (for example a 10 cm square holding about 3 g of water for one brand and 5 g for another), but the winner depends on the definition: per sheet, per square or per gram of towel. | Thicker, quilted or two-ply towels hold more water between their fibres. | Depends on the data: Supported only if Brand X is highest with ranges that do not overlap. | Brands labelled A, B, C by the teacher; tell the group which letter is "Brand X" only after testing if you want a blind test. Same square size, same dip time (10 s) and drip time (10 s). |
| **12. Faster in the morning than after lunch** | Usually a small difference (about 0.01 to 0.02 s) inside the ranges. People are often slightly faster in the second session because of practice. | Alertness changes during the day, but sleep, practice and attention matter more for this test. | Not enough evidence yet. | Needs two time slots (see Week 7 notes). Half the participants should be tested first in the morning and half first after lunch if possible. Same dropper and the Week 3 method. |

**Teacher prep before the lesson (about 40 minutes)**
- Build each group's tray from its approval stub and tray label. Share out thermometers in advance (about 12 in the class); groups that need many can read one thermometer in several cups in turn.
- Plan A: collect ice on the day; make the freezing mixture for claim 1 ten minutes before the lesson; keep the rest of the ice in the cool box.
- Take fizzy drink bottles (claim 10) out of the fridge in the morning so they reach room temperature. Relabel paper towel brands A, B, C (claim 11) and keep the key.
- Print word lists and recall sheets (claim 7), number hunt sheets (claim 8), and the participant script cards (claims 7, 8, 9, 12). Charge your device and test the headphones (claim 7).

**Hazards and controls**
- Ice and salt mixtures are cold enough to hurt skin. Wear gloves. Handle tubes and cups, never the mixture.
- 冰盐混合物温度很低，会冻伤皮肤。请戴手套。只拿试管或杯子，不要接触混合物。
- Warm water can scald. Only the teacher prepares and pours warm water, no hotter than about 60 °C. Cups and bottles of warm water stay on the tray.
- 温水可能烫伤。只有老师可以准备和倒温水，温度不超过约60 °C。装有温水的杯子和瓶子要留在托盘上。
- No eating or drinking. Nothing used in an investigation is eaten or drunk, including ice, salt and fizzy drinks.
- 禁止饮食。调查中使用的任何物品都不能吃或喝，包括冰块、盐和汽水。
- Glass thermometers break easily. Never stir with one or pull one out of ice. If one breaks, step back and tell the teacher.
- 玻璃温度计容易破碎。不要用温度计搅拌，也不要把温度计从冰中硬拔出来。如果温度计破了，请后退并告诉老师。
- Spills make floors slippery. Wipe up spills at once.
- 洒出的水会使地面湿滑。洒出的水要立即擦干。
- Human participants: taking part is voluntary, no pressure, codes not names, anyone can stop or withdraw their results.
- 涉及参与者时：参与完全自愿，不施压，用编号代替姓名，任何人都可以随时停止或撤回自己的数据。
- Tape measures can snap back. Retract slowly and keep the metal end away from faces.
- 卷尺可能快速回弹。请慢慢收回卷尺，金属端远离脸部。

**Clean-up**
- Ice and salt: tip into the bucket; the teacher pours it away with plenty of running water.
- Fizzy drinks and warm water: the teacher pours them away. Bottles into recycling.
- Count thermometers back (about 12), rulers and tape measures; wipe trays and tables; wash hands.
- Data sheets stay on the table until the teacher has photographed or collected them.
