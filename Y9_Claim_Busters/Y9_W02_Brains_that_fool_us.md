## 1. Lesson plan (one page, for the teacher)

- **Big idea:** Our brains take shortcuts that can fool us, so scientists measure, compare groups and try to prove their own ideas wrong.
- **Learning goals:**
  - I can show with measurements that my perception can be wrong, and explain why scientists measure instead of trusting their eyes.
  - I can explain anchoring and confirmation bias using evidence from today's class tests.

**Key words (8)**

| English | 中文 | Student-friendly meaning |
|---|---|---|
| bias | 偏差（偏见） | A pull that pushes our thinking or results in one direction. |
| perception | 知觉（感知） | How our brain understands what our senses tell it. |
| optical illusion | 视错觉 | A picture that tricks our eyes and brain. |
| measure | 测量 | Find a value using a tool, such as a ruler. |
| estimate | 估计（估算） | A careful guess at a number. |
| anchoring | 锚定效应 | When a number we saw first pulls our guess towards it. |
| confirmation bias | 确认偏差 | Looking only for evidence that agrees with what we already think. |
| mean | 平均值 | Add up all the values, then divide by how many there are. |

**Timeline**

| Phase | Min | Teacher does | Students do | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do Now | 4 | Hands out anchoring slips from a pre-shuffled pile (Version 1 and 2 alternate) as students arrive. Says only "Answer alone, in silence, then fold it." Collects slips into the group envelopes. Shows the retrieval question. | Answer the slip silently and fold it. Then answer the retrieval question (claim type and verdict word). | Slides 1 to 2, anchoring slips, envelopes |
| Hook | 3 | Shows the Muller-Lyer lines. Vote: A longer, B longer, same? Does not reveal. Quietly sorts slips into Version 1 and Version 2 piles using the code in the corner. | Vote, then predict on the worksheet. | Slide 3 |
| Learn | 7 | Perception vs measurement; what bias means; how to measure to the nearest millimetre at eye level. Does not mention anchoring yet. | Repeat key words; practise reading a ruler on the slide. | Slides 4 to 6 |
| Do (Part A) | 7 | Gives out illusion sheets. Circulates: "Measure, don't look!" Hands the two piles of slips and the tally sheet to two quick-finishing Recorders with a calculator. | In pairs: predict, then measure the illusions; complete Core. Two volunteers calculate the class means. | Slide 7, illusion sheet, rulers, worksheet Core, tally sheet |
| Do (Part B) | 11 | Runs the 2-4-6 game from the script. Writes every test on the board in YES and NO columns. Reveals the rule and names confirmation bias. | Groups test number sequences, record on the group card, try to find the rule. | Slides 8 to 9, 2-4-6 group card |
| Think and Reflect | 8 | Reveals the anchoring trick and the two class means; compares with the true length (about 6,300 km). Asks "How do scientists protect themselves from bias?" Collects exit tickets. | Pair talk (any language), share with a frame; Stretch or Challenge if time; exit ticket and logbook line. | Slides 10 to 12, worksheet |

**Tiers**
- **Core:** predict and measure the illusions; gap fill about perception and bias.
- **Stretch:** record and explain the class anchoring results; explain why "add 2" seemed right in the 2-4-6 game.
- **Challenge:** explain how one extreme estimate affects the mean and choose a better average; explain anchoring in a shop price; plan how to avoid confirmation bias when testing a claim.

**Language support**
- Frames: "My eyes said ___, but the ruler showed ___." / "The data suggests ___; however, ___." / "This could also be explained by ___."
- Chinese talk is welcome in pair talk and in the 2-4-6 group discussions; tests are called out in English numbers.
- Vocabulary traps: "bias" is often heard as "unfair person"; here it is any pull in one direction, including in data. 偏差 and 偏见 are both used: 偏差 for results, 偏见 for people. "Estimate" is not a wild guess.

**Misconceptions to watch for**
- "Clever people are not fooled." Everyone is fooled by the illusions, even after learning the trick.
- "If many tests say yes, the idea is proven." The 2-4-6 game shows that lots of agreeing tests can still miss the truth.
- "A ruler gives an exact answer." Measuring has uncertainty too (about ±1 mm with a 30 cm ruler).

**Safety:** No practical hazards beyond normal classroom rules.

**If time is short:** cut the bonus illusion C; run the 2-4-6 game for 3 rounds instead of 5; do the anchoring reveal with the teacher computing the means. If class anchoring data is unusable (for example, most students already know the length), use the sample data in the answer key.

**Assessment and handover links**
- Exit ticket: can the student name a bias and a protection against it?
- Logbook line (last minute): What I found 我发现了什么: "My brain was fooled when ___."
- Bias reflection (My thinking box) is the series output for this week.

## 4. Answer key (for the teacher)

**Core 1 (true values)**

| Illusion | Most people predict | True measurements | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lines A and B (Muller-Lyer) | B looks longer | A = 80 mm, B = 80 mm (accept 79 to 81 mm) | 0 mm (accept up to 2 mm because of reading uncertainty) |
| Circles C and D (Ebbinghaus) | D looks bigger | C = 20 mm, D = 20 mm diameter (accept 19 to 21 mm) | 0 mm |
| Bonus: Lines E and F (vertical-horizontal) | E (vertical) looks longer | E = 60 mm, F = 60 mm (accept 59 to 61 mm) | 0 mm |

"Were my eyes right?" is usually No. If a student says "same" and measured the same, Yes is correct; praise them for suspicion.

**Core 2:** a) perception b) optical illusion c) bias d) measure e) mm (millimetre)

**Stretch 1.** Class results vary. Expected pattern: Version 2 (10,000 km) mean higher than Version 1 (1,000 km) mean.
Sample data (use if class data is unusable; show it as "Sample data from another class"):
- Version 1 (anchor 1,000 km), 12 estimates: 1,500; 2,000; 3,000; 1,200; 2,500; 4,000; 1,800; 3,000; 2,000; 5,000; 1,500; 2,500. Total 30,000. Mean = 30,000 ÷ 12 = **2,500 km**. Median 2,250 km.
- Version 2 (anchor 10,000 km), 12 estimates: 8,000; 6,000; 9,000; 12,000; 7,000; 5,000; 8,500; 10,000; 6,500; 9,000; 7,500; 4,000. Total 92,500. Mean = 92,500 ÷ 12 = **about 7,708 km** (7,710 to 3 significant figures). Median 7,750 km.
- Difference between the means: about 5,208 km.
Model explanation: "Group 2's estimates were higher because they saw the number 10,000 first, and it pulled their guesses towards it, even though it was not a clue. This is called anchoring." Excellent adds: "Both groups were pulled away from the true value of about 6,300 km, in opposite directions."
If most students knew the answer (6,300 km is taught in geography), the means may be close together: model "The data suggests anchoring had little effect here; however, many of us already knew the answer, so this was not a fair test of anchoring."

**Stretch 2 (model):** "It seemed right because every test we tried got a 'yes', since sequences that add 2 are also increasing. A better test would have been 1, 2, 3 (or 5, 10, 50), because if the rule were 'add 2' the answer would be 'no', so it could prove our idea wrong."

**Challenge 1 (model):** "One extreme value of 100,000 km adds 100,000 to the total, so it pulls the mean up a lot (with the sample data, the mean would rise from about 7,708 km to about 15,208 km if it replaced the 10,000 km estimate). The median, the middle value when the estimates are put in order, is better, because one extreme value hardly changes it." Accept "remove it as an outlier" only if the student says the rule should be decided before looking at the data.

**Challenge 2 (model):** "¥500 is the anchor. Compared with ¥500, ¥199 feels cheap, even if the item is only worth ¥150. The first number pulls our judgement of what is a good price."

**Challenge 3 (model):** "They might remember the times they used the pen and did well, and forget the times they did badly. That is confirmation bias. To test it properly, they should do the same memory test many times with and without the pen and compare the mean scores, ideally without knowing which pen is 'lucky'." (This sets up Week 5.)

**My thinking (Secure model):** "Our senses can be fooled even when we know the trick, so we cannot rely on how things look. Scientists use measurements and tools, compare results and check each other, because a ruler is not fooled."

**Exit ticket:** 1. Any of: the lines or circles looked different sizes; the Do Now number pulled my estimate; I only tested sequences I expected to be "yes". 2. Matching protection: measure with a tool; compare two groups or hide the anchor; try tests that could prove your idea wrong. 3. **b) 1, 2, 3** (it follows the real rule but not "add 2", so a "yes" proves "add 2" is wrong; a) and c) can only confirm).

### 6d. 2-4-6 game: teacher script

Create an A4 teacher script card for Year 9 Week 2, Claim Busters series, using the Think Like a Scientist design system. Print spec: A4, 1 copy for the teacher.

**Secret rule (do not show):** any three numbers in increasing order. Each number must be bigger than the one before. Negative numbers, zero, decimals, fractions and huge numbers are all allowed. Equal numbers (5, 5, 5) and decreasing numbers get "No".

**1. Set-up (1 minute). Say:**
"I have a secret rule for sets of three numbers. The numbers 2, 4, 6 follow my rule. Your job is to find the rule. You can test any three numbers. I will only answer 'Yes, that follows my rule' or 'No, it does not.' Before each test, write your prediction on your group card. When your group is sure of the rule, write it in the box and hold up the card. You cannot ask me questions. The only way to get information is to test."

**2. Testing rounds (about 6 minutes, up to 5 rounds).**
- Go round the groups in order. Each group gives one set of three numbers per round.
- Write every set on the board in the YES column or the NO column. Answer only "Yes" or "No". Keep your face neutral.
- If a group holds up a rule that is wrong (for example "add 2", "even numbers", "go up by the same amount"), say only: "That is not my rule. Keep testing."
- If a group holds up "any increasing numbers" or "each number bigger than the last", say: "Keep that secret until the end." Count them.
- Expected: most early tests are 8, 10, 12 or 20, 22, 24 or 100, 102, 104, all "Yes". The NO column usually stays almost empty. Do not comment on this yet.

**3. Reveal (2 minutes). Say:**
"The rule is: any three numbers in increasing order. Look at the board. How many tests are in the NO column? Most of us tested numbers we expected to get a 'yes'. That felt good, but it taught us almost nothing. This is called confirmation bias, 确认偏差: looking for evidence that agrees with what we already think. If you think the rule is 'add 2', the clever test is 1, 2, 3 or 3, 7, 50. A 'yes' to those proves your idea wrong. Scientists try to prove their own ideas wrong."

**4. Sharp question for fast thinkers (optional, 1 minute). Ask:**
"Even now, can you be completely sure the rule is not 'increasing numbers less than one million'?" (No: you can never test every possibility. Scientific conclusions can change with new evidence. This is why "Not enough evidence yet" is an honest verdict.)
